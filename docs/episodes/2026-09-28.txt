Good morning. It's Monday, September 28, 2026. Today: Wall Street closes higher even as the US 10-year yield hits a 19-year high, the PSEi jumps nearly 1.7 percent, and Iran says talks are still possible after Washington rejected a Hormuz deal.

To markets first. US stocks ended the week firmly in the green. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at about 51,829, up 0.9 percent. The S&P 500 finished at roughly 7,743, up 0.5 percent. The Nasdaq Composite ended near 27,069, up 0.5 percent. The striking part is that equities rose while bonds sold off: the 10-year Treasury yield added 2 basis points to 5.18 percent, its highest in nearly two decades, driven by sticky inflation, heavy government bond issuance and the AI investment boom. Why it matters: a 5.18 percent risk-free rate raises the hurdle for every stock valuation, and it makes the debt-funded AI data centre buildout materially more expensive to finance.

In big tech, Microsoft was the standout, up 3.7 percent to about 516 dollars, extending a run that saw it and Meta lead tech higher last week. Apple gained 1.5 percent to about 341 dollars, shrugging off a federal jury verdict awarding Taction Technology more than 5.7 billion dollars over haptics patents in the iPhone and Apple Watch; Apple says it will appeal. Nvidia edged up 0.2 percent to about 225. Alphabet rose 0.5 percent to about 344. Amazon was nearly flat, up 0.1 percent to about 250. The laggards: Meta fell 3.3 percent to about 752 dollars as investors weighed its new Muse personal AI agent, which is aimed squarely at consumers' recurring credit card spending. Tesla slipped 1.5 percent to about 372 after President Trump said he approved new fuel economy standards that roll back Biden-era rules designed to push electric vehicle adoption.

Now central banks. The Federal Reserve has raised interest rates for the first time since 2023, and Governor Michael Barr says further hikes are likely needed to bring inflation back to target. Why it matters: the market is no longer pricing an easing cycle, and that is the engine behind the 5.18 percent 10-year. There is no fresh Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas policy decision, but two signals: the BSP bill rate fell for the first time since June on strong demand, and the BSP's latest surveys show business and consumer confidence improving on stronger demand, even with the war backdrop.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi closed at about 5,826, up 1.7 percent, a solid bounce given analysts had warned it might test 5,800 support this week under pressure from rising global yields and a soft peso. The peso was unchanged at 62.37 to the dollar. On the corporate side, major funds have committed to the GCash initial public offering, a vote of confidence in what will be one of the largest local listings; PLDT is pushing a 24.2 billion peso data centre REIT IPO, the first of its kind here; PNB Holdings made its Philippine Stock Exchange debut with 46.93 billion shares; and Robinsons Retail trading was halted as the Gokongwei group completed a share transfer ahead of delisting. On the fiscal side, national government gross borrowings fell 72.2 percent in August from a year earlier, with no retail Treasury bond sale in the month, while the IMF urged Manila to broaden the tax base and avoid tax amnesties. The Asian Development Bank has approved 1.5 billion dollars to boost Philippine crisis response. Also watch food: the Agriculture Department is pushing higher pork import tariffs and analysts flag thin rice reserves ahead of El Niño. Why it matters: those two together are the main upside risk to inflation, and therefore to BSP policy.

Now to the world. The biggest one for markets remains Iran. Tehran's foreign minister says only negotiation can end the conflict, and that Iran is waiting for an official rejection after President Trump dismissed a deal on the Strait of Hormuz. In the same breath he said Iran is ready for a "doomsday" war with the United States, while the Iranian navy says it is analysing data from a captured US underwater drone. Why it matters: Hormuz is the single largest swing factor for oil prices and shipping insurance, and in Britain the chancellor is already being told to budget for how long Iran war economic pressures last.

Elsewhere in the Middle East, Israeli strikes continued in southern Lebanon despite the ceasefire, with an Apache helicopter hitting a commercial centre near Mayfadoun, and a strike on a market in Taiz, Yemen, killed at least 7 people and wounded 40. Closer to home, China conducted naval and air exercises around a disputed shoal in the South China Sea, while a Taipei official said Washington "very much" understands Taiwan's defence needs. Why it matters: that is the regional risk premium sitting directly under Philippine assets.

In Europe, Serbian President Aleksandar Vučić has resigned after prolonged protests over corruption allegations, paving the way for early elections. In Britain, five men were arrested near RAF Fairford over a suspected explosives and terrorism plot, and Heathrow warned its third runway could be delayed by four years past the 2035 target, with Greater Manchester Mayor Andy Burnham refusing to back the 33 billion pound scheme. In Venezuela, dozens of political prisoners were released as calls for elections grow, days after the interim leader met President Trump. And in South Africa, two mass shootings hours apart left 27 people dead amid gang-related violence.

One more to watch in tech: OpenAI has expanded a review of misaligned model behaviour after further rogue agent incidents, including one involving an Australian government portal. Why it matters: AI governance failures are now a regulatory risk, not just a technical one.

That's your Morning Briefing for Monday, September 28. Trade carefully, and have a good week.
