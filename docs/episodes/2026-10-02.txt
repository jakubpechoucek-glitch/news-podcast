Good morning. It's Friday, October 2, 2026. Oil near $100 a barrel is the thread running through almost everything today, from Wall Street to the Philippine Stock Exchange.

Let's start with markets. US stocks closed narrowly higher. The Dow Jones Industrial Average finished at about 50,927, up less than 0.1 percent. The S&P 500 closed at roughly 7,666, up 0.2 percent. The Nasdaq Composite ended near 26,872, also up less than 0.1 percent. The move of the day was in bonds: the US 10-year Treasury yield fell 6 basis points to 5.24 percent, pulling back from multiyear highs after a run of heavy selling in government debt. Why it matters: a 10-year above 5 percent is the single biggest constraint on equity valuations right now, and a soft reading there is what kept stocks green.

In big tech, Alphabet was the clear laggard, down 1.7 percent to about 338 dollars. Google launched its latest AI model, Gemini 4, promising advances in coding and cybersecurity, but Wall Street wanted a breakout personal agent and sold the news. Nvidia was the best performer, up 1.1 percent to about 231 dollars. Apple fell 0.8 percent to about 330. Amazon slipped 0.4 percent, Tesla eased 0.2 percent, Meta added 0.1 percent to about 726, and Microsoft was essentially flat at about 513 as former LinkedIn chief Ryan Roslansky becomes the latest in a run of senior departures. Elsewhere, Nike shares dropped on disappointing sales and layoffs tied to its restructuring, and Micron fell despite what analysts called a strong quarter. Boeing avoided a strike after its largest white-collar union approved a new contract.

On central banks, there is real news. Fed officials have washed away market bets on an October rate increase, with the vice chair saying it "may take more time" before hiking again, and Goldman Sachs has pushed its next hike call to December. Neel Kashkari said inflation is "still too high" even after a softer-than-expected PCE print, while calling the labor market "pretty good." Why it matters: the Fed is still in a tightening bias, not an easing one, and that is what's keeping long yields elevated. There's no new rate decision from the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, but the BSP has flagged that September inflation likely hit a three-year high, which effectively shuts the door on near-term easing in Manila.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi fell 0.9 percent to about 5,629, a fourth straight losing session, sinking deeper into the 5,600s as crude nears 100 dollars a barrel and inflation worries build ahead of the September print. The peso firmed slightly, at 62.53 to the dollar, 0.2 percent stronger on the day. Three things to watch. First, financing: the Bureau of the Treasury is considering a retail dollar bond offer before year-end and is eyeing shorter tenors on dollar debt because of high global rates. That matters because the country's net external liabilities widened to 65.6 billion dollars in the second quarter as borrowing rose and reserves shrank. Second, credit is still running hot: bank lending grew 11 percent in August and domestic liquidity hit 20.7 trillion pesos, which complicates the inflation picture. Third, the BSP suspended Coins.ph from accepting cash-ins via banks and e-wallets, a notable enforcement move in digital finance. On the corporate side, Philex Mining expects reduced output at its Padcal mine in Benguet as a workers' strike looms, and Robinsons Retail trading was halted as the Gokongwei group completed a share transfer for delisting.

Now to the world. Oil is the headline. Brent jumped more than 4 percent Thursday after reports the United States is sending a third aircraft carrier strike group to the Middle East. Why it matters: that one move is driving Philippine pump prices, PSEi weakness and the inflation risk the BSP just flagged. Related, President Trump has threatened to ban US diesel exports to ease domestic prices, and Britain is now in talks with EU partners over fuel reserves, with analysts warning Europe's winter energy crunch may already be underway.

On Russia, Vladimir Putin ruled out a ceasefire with Ukraine in a Moscow speech and warned the West that Russia is ready to use every weapon, including nuclear ones, to defend the Kaliningrad exclave, while insisting Moscow is "not threatening anyone." Separately, Russian dissident Garry Kasparov says US officials warned him of a threat to his life. Why it matters: escalation risk around the Baltic is now a live energy and defense trade.

On the Middle East, Saudi Arabia's initial probe into the Flydubai incident says the co-pilot assaulted the captain before landing; both pilots were returned to the UAE with an Emirati security team. Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu said the attacker showed "Islamist radical indoctrination" and intended to down the plane. Meanwhile, a new poll finds nearly 70 percent of Americans say the US-Israeli war on Iran is not worth fighting, a sharp jump since July, which is political pressure the White House can't ignore while it moves carriers into the region.

Two more. Closer to home, the Philippines deployed its largest ship and aircraft to drive away a Chinese vessel in waters near Taiwan, a reminder that the South China Sea risk premium is a domestic story, not just a regional one. And Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey warned that the AI investment boom could trigger market shocks, saying the central bank is watching the waves of cash flowing into AI "very carefully." Why it matters: with the Fed on hold and valuations stretched, an AI-led correction is now an explicit central-bank concern.

Finally, Japan has raised its permanent residency fee by 20 times as part of broader immigration changes, a direct cost increase for Filipino workers and families pursuing long-term status there.

That's your Morning Briefing for Friday, October 2. Watch the September inflation print and the oil tape today.
