Good morning. It's Thursday, November 5, 2026. Today: a post-election surge on Wall Street, oil below 90 dollars, and a Philippine economy absorbing 7.8 percent inflation.

Start with markets. US stocks ripped higher after the midterm results removed a layer of political uncertainty. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at about 53,356, up 3.0 percent. The S&P 500 finished near 7,689, up 1.6 percent — its biggest one-day gain in three months. The Nasdaq Composite ended around 25,737, up 1.4 percent. The US 10-year Treasury yield slipped 6 basis points to 4.89 percent, helped by falling crude. Why it matters: lower yields and a softer dollar are the transmission channel from Washington's politics to Asian currencies and bond markets.

Big tech was mostly the engine. Microsoft rose 2.7 percent to about 521 dollars, Meta gained 2.4 percent to 621, Nvidia added 2.3 percent to 207, Amazon rose 2.1 percent to 251, and Alphabet edged up 0.4 percent to 280. The exceptions: Apple fell 1.9 percent to about 332, and Tesla dropped 1.3 percent to roughly 399. Elon Musk–linked stocks were the clear losers — Tesla and SpaceX shed more than 120 billion dollars in combined market value on fears a Democratic Congress will tighten scrutiny of his businesses, after Musk spent nearly 500 million dollars on the midterms, an individual spending record, with nothing to show for it. Elsewhere, AI data-centre names fell while renewables and healthcare rallied; Qualcomm dropped 7 percent on a weak forecast tied to a memory shortage hitting smartphones; Snap fell more than 10 percent on a revenue miss. Bitcoin rebounded more than 4 percent to above 81,000 dollars. Jim Cramer's caution is worth noting: Wall Street may be reading too much into one session.

On central banks, the Federal Reserve has raised interest rates for the first time since 2023, with inflation at a three-year high, and attention now turns to Friday's US jobs report, which will shape the December decision. Here at home, the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas has also tightened. GlobalSource Partners says the BSP should not be judged by the peso's reaction — the hike targets inflation, not the exchange rate. Separately, the BSP governor has flagged concern over the borrowing frenzy among AI firms and urged central banks to study artificial intelligence's impact on monetary policy and financial stability.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi rebounded 1.2 percent to about 5,764 on bargain hunting and relief over the US election outcome, after two straight losing sessions. The peso firmed 0.29 percent to 63.18 to the dollar. The dominant domestic number is inflation at 7.8 percent, which strengthens the case for further BSP tightening — and squeezes household budgets heading into the holidays. Energy is the pressure point: the government has declared a national energy emergency as the Iran war threatens Asian supply, and the cost is visible in corporate results. Philippine Airlines swung to a net loss of 159 million dollars for the nine months after fuel expenses jumped 339.4 million dollars. Petron's profit nearly halved to 5.4 billion pesos. On the brighter side, PEZA expects record investment approvals of up to 320 billion pesos this year, Filinvest REIT's nine-month profit rose 28 percent to 1.3 billion pesos, and Monde Nissin's third-quarter profit edged up 1.8 percent to 2.29 billion pesos. Politically, Malacañang has confirmed President Trump will attend the ASEAN Summit in Manila from November 16 to 18, with Manila still awaiting a US reply on the Pax Silica deal.

Turning to India. The Nifty 50 closed at about 23,811, up 1.6 percent, and the Sensex at roughly 76,918, up 1.6 percent — a fifth straight day of gains, with the Nifty at a two-month high and the Sensex reclaiming its 200-day moving average. The rupee strengthened 0.23 percent to 94.53 to the dollar. GIFT Nifty was pointing to another strong open, trading around 24,300. The logic is straightforward: a softer dollar, cooling Treasury yields and cheaper crude all support foreign inflows into Indian equities and ease imported inflation. That matters because foreign investor holdings have fallen to an all-time low of 14 percent, and New Delhi is weighing a tax cut to win them back. Despite the rebound, Samvat 2082 is on track to be the worst in 15 years for Indian benchmarks. From the Reserve Bank of India, no rate move, but it has set up a Technical Consultative Committee on financial markets to deepen money, forex and government securities markets, and has launched three household surveys on inflation, jobs and spending. Dollar hedging has slumped as the RBI cracks down on speculation. SEBI, meanwhile, has eased KYC rules to let NRIs and foreign nationals onboard digitally from overseas.

Now the wider world. The biggest story remains the US midterms. Democrats have flipped seven Senate seats, nearly 30 House districts, five governorships and up to 12 state legislative chambers; Republicans have flipped none. President Trump has acknowledged the Iran war "largely" caused the wipeout but says he "had to do it", while Vice President Vance blamed the war directly. Crude fell below 90 dollars a barrel on his comments. Why it matters: a Democratic Congress means sharper scrutiny of tariffs, the Iran and Ukraine wars and crypto legislation, even though the president retains broad foreign-policy powers. Canada is reading it as leverage, with US trade talks back on and hopes of tariff relief.

In Ukraine, Kyiv says bodycam footage shows a Russian double-tap bombing on a village on Monday that killed at least 40 people, with 30 more possibly still under rubble. In Israel, President Herzog has begun consultations with parties after an election deadlock, warning that not every promise can be kept if another election is to be avoided. In Tehran, demonstrators burned a giant banner of Trump, Vance and Netanyahu — a reminder that de-escalation in the Gulf, and therefore the oil price, remains fragile.

In the UK, the Bank of England is expected to raise rates from 3.75 to 4 percent, its first increase in more than three years, and the pound has climbed to its highest level against a basket of currencies since the Brexit referendum. London and Brussels have also agreed a draft deal scrapping red tape on food exports ahead of a summit this month. In corporate distress, Australia's 188-year-old department store David Jones has collapsed into receivership, and Poundland's owner Gordon Brothers is in crunch talks with the UK business secretary. In Africa, Ethiopia has expelled six Sudanese diplomats and ordered a consulate closed, while Aliko Dangote opened a major fuel storage terminal in Namibia aimed at securing Southern Africa's supply.

That's your Morning Briefing for Thursday, November 5 — watch Friday's US jobs report and the Bank of England decision.
