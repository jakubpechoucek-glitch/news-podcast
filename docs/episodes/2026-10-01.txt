Good morning. It's Thursday, October 1st, 2026. Today: softer US inflation but a Federal Reserve still arguing about another hike, the PSEi at a 2026 low, and Russia's biggest strike yet on Ukraine's power grid.

To the markets. Wall Street closed split. The Dow Jones fell 0.9 percent to about 50,906. The S&P 500 slipped 0.3 percent to roughly 7,652. But the Nasdaq Composite rose 0.2 percent to about 26,861. The US 10-year Treasury yield climbed 4 basis points to 5.29 percent, and that rising yield is the simplest explanation for the split: long-duration borrowing costs are squeezing the broader index while a handful of AI winners carry the tech benchmark.

Big tech was mostly green. Apple rose 1.1 percent to about 333 dollars. Amazon gained 1.0 percent to about 249. Alphabet added 0.9 percent to about 344 after rolling out Gemini 4 Argon, its most advanced model, pitched at coding, cybersecurity and complex professional work. Microsoft rose 0.8 percent to about 513, Tesla 0.6 percent to about 355, and Nvidia 0.5 percent to about 228, which analysts flag as near a technical breakout level. The exception was Meta, down 1.8 percent to about 725 — profit-taking after its best month since 2022 on AI momentum. Also worth noting: Micron beat on earnings and guided higher, with data centre revenue up elevenfold, and the stock is up more than 500 percent in a year. Why it matters: the AI trade is still the only reliable source of upside in this market.

On central banks. The Fed's preferred gauge came in cool: core personal consumption expenditures inflation was 3.0 percent in August, well under the 3.3 percent consensus, with the headline measure dipping to 3.4 percent. New York Fed President John Williams hinted the next rate increase can wait, while Governor Michael Barr said the Fed may not be done raising. Note the framing: the debate is about the timing of the next hike, not a cut.

Now to the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas. No policy meeting, but a significant signal: the BSP projects September inflation landed between 6.4 and 7.4 percent, which would be a three-year high. It urged households to manage spending, with inflation in BARMM already at 8.8 percent. Separately, the BSP blocked inbound transfers into DCPay, the e-wallet behind Coins.ph, halting cash-ins via banks and other wallets. Why it matters: a 7-handle print kills any remaining hope of easing and keeps the pressure on the peso.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi fell 1.0 percent to about 5,679 — a 2026 low and, by some counts, a 10-month low — with inflation worries, climbing bond yields and firmer oil prices all cited. The peso weakened 0.25 percent to 62.68 to the dollar. Why it matters: domestic equities are now pricing a stagflation-tinged scenario, not just a soft patch.

Three other Philippine stories. First, the government raised 84.9 billion pesos from its retail bond offer, six times oversubscribed — proof that peso-denominated yield is in demand even as equities sell off. Second, the Asian Development Bank approved 1.5 billion dollars to strengthen the Philippines' crisis response capacity, useful fiscal headroom given this year's disaster bill. Third, corporate moves: ABS-CBN shareholders approved a 6-billion-peso placement from the Lopez family and a new investor, with the company staying Lopez-led; Robinsons Retail trading was suspended as the Gokongwei group completed a share transfer for delisting; and Century Properties won approval to merge with PHirst Park Homes. On the data side, banks' foreign currency loans rose 5.6 percent to 16.31 billion dollars in the second quarter, driven by exporters.

Now to the world. Russia launched its largest attack on Ukraine's energy infrastructure since spring, hitting 11 regions overnight, with residential buildings and railways also targeted, according to Ukraine's prime minister. Why it matters: grid attacks ahead of winter raise the odds of another European energy-price scare.

In the Gulf, Qatari mediators are pressing a peace deal between the United States and Iran. Crude oil exports through the Strait of Hormuz have recovered to pre-war levels, though refined fuel shipments remain constrained and the recovery depends on the US military escorting tankers in the Persian Gulf. Why it matters: that is a fragile, policy-dependent supply line, and Philippine inflation is directly exposed to it.

The last British and American troops have left Iraq, ending the anti-Islamic State coalition mission under a deal agreed two years ago and meeting a key demand of Baghdad. Elsewhere in the region, Israeli attacks across residential areas of Gaza killed at least eight civilians on Wednesday, and a gas pipeline explosion in Syria knocked three power plants offline, forcing longer rationing hours.

On trade, Beijing's Commerce Ministry warned it must respond firmly if the European Union imposes restrictions on Chinese businesses or products. Why it matters: another front in the tariff war would hit Asian supply chains, including Philippine electronics.

Two more. In Ethiopia, escalating fighting in Tigray killed 52 civilians according to a medic speaking to the BBC, with violence spreading to neighbouring regions and tourists stranded in Lalibela. And in Nepal, around 30 people have died in landslides and heavy rain, with trekkers helicoptered off mountains a month after catastrophic flooding.

Finally, on AI regulation: the US Federal Trade Commission has opened a probe into AI companies over rogue agents and longer-term risks, OpenAI's Sam Altman declined to testify at a Senate subcommittee hearing on the subject, and Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey said regulating AI is "not the right place to start," calling instead for rigorous testing and safeguards. Why it matters: regulatory risk is the one variable the AI trade has not yet priced.

That's your Morning Briefing for Thursday, October 1st. Trade carefully.
