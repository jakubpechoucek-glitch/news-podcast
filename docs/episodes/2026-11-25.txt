Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 25, 2026. Today: the AI trade cracks again, the Fed leans toward another hike, the peso slips past 63, India rallies hard, and Washington lines up new tariffs.

To the markets. Wall Street split badly. The Dow closed at about 50,019, up 0.9 percent, while the S&P 500 slipped 0.5 percent to about 7,201 and the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.5 percent to about 23,734. That gap tells you everything: money rotating out of artificial intelligence names and into everything else. The US 10-year Treasury yield was close to flat at 4.90 percent, down 1 basis point.

Nvidia was the wrecking ball, down 5.9 percent to about 155 dollars, after the US attorney general dodged questions about an investigation into the company and lawmakers demanded answers about President Trump's role in reinstating chief executive Jensen Huang. Trump has since said he would be willing to pardon Huang if needed. Huang, for his part, told staff Nvidia does not need a succession plan. Why it matters: the single most important stock in the index now carries political risk on top of valuation risk, and AI debt markets wobbled on Tuesday as a result.

Elsewhere in big tech, Apple rose 1.4 percent to about 354 dollars and Microsoft added 0.7 percent to about 496. Alphabet fell 1.1 percent to about 279, Amazon lost 1.2 percent to about 252, Meta dropped 1.3 percent to about 590, and Tesla was effectively flat, down 0.2 percent to about 385. Dell bucked the trend after earnings beat on AI server demand, with sales more than doubling year on year and a 25 billion dollar buyback authorised.

Central banks. The Fed is not finished. New York Fed President John Williams said inflation is still too high and signalled higher rates are likely over time, while minutes showed most regional Fed boards wanted to raise the discount rate in October. Markets now put the odds of a December hike near 97 percent. Bill Ackman says the Fed is about to make another mistake. Gold fell more than 2 percent on those rate expectations.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi closed at about 5,435, down 0.4 percent, pausing after a near-2 percent rebound that was driven by Bangko Sentral signalling less aggressive tightening as growth slows. The peso weakened 0.5 percent to 63.68 to the dollar. BSP Governor's own words on the currency: "it's scary." He also said a pause on rate hikes in December is "possible but unlikely," and defended the central bank's earlier gold sales as the metal hits new highs. Why it matters: higher-for-longer here and in Washington keeps peso borrowing expensive. You can see that in the bond market, where BPI priced 5 billion pesos of sustainability bonds at 7.251 percent and Bank of Commerce raised 17.4 billion pesos in a dual-tranche deal. On trade, the Palace says there is no deal to buy US energy and farm products, pushing back on reports ahead of Washington's tariff announcement. Some relief at the pump: diesel and gasoline prices fell again, and LPG is seen declining further in December.

Turning to India. A powerful rebound. The Nifty 50 closed at about 25,796, up 1.8 percent, and the Sensex at about 83,794, up 1.7 percent, adding roughly 2.2 lakh crore rupees in market value and clawing back Monday's steep slide. Three drivers: foreign inflows, falling crude, and a firmer currency. The rupee strengthened 0.3 percent to 92.61 to the dollar, helped by Brent crude down about 1.3 percent. Why it matters: India is being treated as the reverse-AI trade, a market that benefits when global investors rotate out of expensive semiconductors.

From the Reserve Bank of India, no policy move yet, with bond yields rangebound ahead of the coming rate decision. The RBI did propose new digital payment safeguards letting customers switch off cards, cap limits and control UPI, and cleared SBI Mutual Fund to take up to 9.99 percent of South Indian Bank. India's largest pension funds are pivoting into government bonds as equity allocations near their caps. And the Mahanadi Coalfields IPO allotment lands today, with listing planned for Friday.

Now the world and geopolitics. First, trade. The United States is preparing to unveil new tariffs hitting many of its biggest trading partners, including China and the European Union. Why it matters: for export-dependent Asia, including the Philippines, this is the single biggest macro risk into December.

Second, Huawei. A New York jury convicted the Chinese telecom giant of bank fraud, money laundering and conspiracy to steal trade secrets, while acquitting it on racketeering. Coming alongside the Nvidia drama, it hardens the technology confrontation between Washington and Beijing, which analysts now say is about talent as much as chips.

Third, energy and the Gulf. Houthi fighters struck the Riyadh tower of Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund, the PIF, injuring 11 people. It's the second attack on that building in six days. Oil did not spike, with Brent down 1.3 percent, but any escalation against Saudi financial infrastructure is a live risk to crude.

Fourth, Asia's tech selloff. Japanese, South Korean and Taiwanese markets dived, with SoftBank down 15 percent, as AI doubts rippled across the region. Why it matters: Asian semiconductor supply chains amplify every swing in the US AI trade.

And briefly: leaked Russian data suggests almost 500,000 deaths from the war in Ukraine, a figure that has reshaped the country's mortality patterns. In Britain, regulator Ofgem is set to announce a new energy price cap that will push domestic gas bills significantly higher in January. In Venezuela, protesters clashed with police near the presidential palace over the minimum wage. An Israeli airstrike on an apartment building in Gaza's Shati refugee camp killed at least four people, and the Philippines reaffirmed its support for a two-state solution as Palestine marked 38 years since its declaration of independence. The World Health Organization chief urged an end to the US fuel blockade on Cuba, with the UN allocating 2 million dollars for medical supplies. And Kilmar Ábrego García has left the United States for Spain, closing a deportation fight that became a flash point in the US immigration debate.

Two quick ones to close. The UAE says it's targeting 7 to 8 percent growth next year after the IMF lifted its forecast. And China's Xiamen Airlines agreed to buy 50 Boeing 737-8 jets, a deal with a catalogue price near 7.4 billion dollars, a rare piece of US-China commercial good news.

That's your Morning Briefing for Wednesday, November 25. Trade carefully, and we'll see you tomorrow.
