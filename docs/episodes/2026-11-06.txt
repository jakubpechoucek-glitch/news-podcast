Good morning. It's Friday, November 6, 2026. Here's what matters: a brutal AI-led selloff on Wall Street has spilled into Asia, Philippine inflation has hit a three-year high, and Washington is turning up the heat on Iran.

To markets. US stocks had one of their worst sessions of the year. The Dow closed at about 51,523, down 3.4 percent. The S&P 500 finished near 7,354, down 4.4 percent. The Nasdaq Composite ended around 24,315, down 5.5 percent, which puts it in correction territory. By one tally, roughly 2.3 trillion dollars in market value was wiped out, with the Magnificent Seven alone shedding about 1 trillion dollars on Thursday. The trigger: the Democratic sweep in the midterms, after winning candidates and progressive lawmakers called for a national data centre moratorium and tougher scrutiny of the AI buildout. Why it matters: the AI capital-expenditure story has been the single biggest driver of global equity returns, and it is now a political target.

Money ran to safety. The US 10-year Treasury yield slipped 6 basis points to 4.84 percent.

Among the big names in the latest snapshot, the split was stark. Nvidia fell 3.2 percent to about 200 dollars, and Tesla dropped 3.3 percent to about 385. Tesla and SpaceX have together lost more than 420 billion dollars in combined market value since the midterms. On the other side, Microsoft rose 6.7 percent to about 556, Amazon gained 6.1 percent to 266, Apple added 5.3 percent to about 350, Alphabet rose 3.2 percent to 289, and Meta edged up 1.4 percent to 630. Apple is also seeking about 7 billion dollars from a US dollar bond sale, the latest tech giant tapping debt markets to fund AI expansion.

Asia took the hit hard this morning. South Korea's Kospi slid 8.8 percent in early trade, triggering a circuit breaker, with Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix both down more than 9 percent. Japan's Nikkei lost 6.2 percent and the MSCI Asia Pacific index fell 2.9 percent. TSMC and SoftBank also tumbled.

On central banks: the Federal Reserve has held rates steady, but the signal is hawkish. Richmond's Tom Barkin and Cleveland's Beth Hammack both favour a gradual path of rate hikes from here, citing inflation. One small offset: the average US 30-year mortgage rate fell for a third straight week to 7.18 percent.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi closed at about 5,731, down 0.8 percent, snapping a two-day rally built on bargain-hunting and relief after the US election. The peso firmed 0.5 percent to 62.99 to the dollar, back in the 62 handle.

The big domestic number: October inflation surged to 7.8 percent, a three-year high. ING expects the Bangko Sentral to hike just 25 basis points in December; Barclays warns inflation could touch 8 percent in November and sees another jumbo hike. Why it matters: your borrowing costs are going up, the only question is how fast. Separately, bank lending and domestic liquidity both grew in September, and the BSP governor has flagged concern over the borrowing frenzy among AI firms, arguing central banks must now factor AI into price stability.

Two more to watch. JP Morgan will add Philippine peso-denominated government bonds to its key emerging-market bond index starting in 2027, phased over 10 months, which should pull in passive foreign flows. And President Marcos has nominated Justice Zalameda to lead the Supreme Court. On earnings, San Miguel Food and Beverage's nine-month net income fell 9 percent to 30.6 billion pesos on slower consumer demand, while Century Pacific's third-quarter profit dropped 46 percent on higher costs and taxes.

Turning to India. Indian markets bucked the global selloff. The Nifty 50 closed at about 23,961, up 0.6 percent, and the Sensex at roughly 77,301, up 0.5 percent, a sixth straight winning session. Foreign funds have been the engine, pumping in nearly 14,975 crore rupees. The rupee strengthened 0.25 percent to 94.52 to the dollar. That said, GIFT Nifty was pointing about 0.65 percent lower, so today's open looks soft given the Wall Street and Asia rout.

Policy-wise, New Delhi is weighing a tax cut to win back foreign investors, whose holdings in Indian equities have fallen to an all-time low of around 14 percent. Why it matters: the current rally is foreign-flow driven, and that flow is fragile. From the Reserve Bank of India, foreign exchange inflows under its swap facility have reached 72.85 billion dollars, and the RBI has released draft priority sector lending norms that keep broad targets intact while proposing a 100 crore rupee cap on bank loans for agricultural infrastructure. Markets regulator SEBI has also aligned currency derivatives rules with RBI norms, capping unhedged positions at 5 million dollars. And Prime Minister Modi used a speech to declare India is no longer among the so-called Fragile Five.

To the world. Start with Iran. President Trump has warned the US may have to "destroy" the rest of Iran, saying people will "know soon enough." Oil trading has been choppy through the week, and any escalation is the clearest upside risk to crude and therefore to Philippine import costs.

On Russia and Ukraine, Trump met a Russian envoy and again said a major deal is "very close," without specifics. In US domestic politics, the midterm fallout continues: DSA-backed Nithya Raman defeated incumbent Karen Bass to become Los Angeles mayor and the city's first South Asian mayor, meaning America's two largest cities will both be led by democratic socialists. Melat Kiros becomes the first Generation Z woman elected to Congress. Analysts also warn the Democratic sweep raises risks around Trump's ability to hold together the fragile trade truce with China, while Taiwan says it expects continued US arms sales support.

In Europe, interest rates were hiked to 4 percent from 3.75 percent, the first increase in more than three years, with one more hike seen as likely. France saw protest numbers surge as unions joined strikes over the cost of living; the interior ministry estimates 600,000 people took part. And for the second time in a week, hoax bomb threats hit European airlines and airports, disrupting at least 21 flights across London, Dublin and Helsinki.

Elsewhere, Paraguay's President Santiago Peña has resigned after a bank collapse and an impeachment vote in the lower house. South Africa's ANC is tracking new lows in local elections, at about 32 percent with more than 60 percent of votes counted. Turkey's parliament has ratified a Mecca defence agreement, laying the groundwork for deeper Turkish involvement in Saudi Arabia's conflict with the Houthis. And in the US, California Governor Gavin Newsom commuted the sentences of all 548 people on the state's death row, the largest mass clemency in American history.

That's your Morning Briefing. Trade carefully and have a good Friday.
