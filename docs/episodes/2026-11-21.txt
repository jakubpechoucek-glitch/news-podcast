Good morning. It's Saturday, November 21, 2026. Today: a brutal week for the AI trade on Wall Street, the Philippine market at a six-year low, and India quietly going the other way.

Start with the United States, where Friday was ugly. The Nasdaq Composite closed at about 22,618, down 5.3 percent. The S&P 500 finished near 6,910, down 3.7 percent, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average at about 48,147, down 1.9 percent. Both the S&P and the Dow are now in correction territory, and the Nasdaq 100 posted its worst week since March 2020, wiping out its gains for 2026. Why it matters: the AI trade that carried global equities all year is unwinding, and it is dragging everything with it.

The epicentre was Nvidia, down 14.0 percent to 125.53, capping its longest weekly losing streak since the 2008 financial crisis. Once the most valuable company in the world, it now ranks fourth, behind Apple, Microsoft and Alphabet. Tesla fell 7.6 percent to 345.82, Meta 5.9 percent to 560.84, Amazon 4.1 percent to 246.82, Microsoft 2.8 percent to 484.52, Alphabet 2.7 percent to 265.29, and Apple 1.9 percent to 342.56. Anthropic, which just pulled off the largest IPO on record at 81 billion dollars, saw its debut rally fade, falling more than 7 percent in pre-market trade and deepening the gloom. Investors rotated into value stocks and into bonds: the US 10-year Treasury yield fell 14 basis points to 4.91 percent. Gold logged its worst week since March, hit by the metals sell-off and a hawkish Federal Reserve.

On central banks, the Fed has raised interest rates for the first time since 2023, and the debate now is about pace, not direction. Governor Lisa Cook says more hikes are needed to bring inflation down and has questioned whether one is enough. Axios reports policymakers broadly want another hike next month but disagree on size, with a half-point move openly discussed. Chairman Warsh has asked colleagues to say less in public; they are saying more. Why it matters: a tightening Fed into an AI-led equity slump is the single biggest risk to global markets right now. There was no fresh Bangko Sentral rate decision this week, but Bank of the Philippine Islands argues the BSP should keep raising cautiously despite sluggish growth, while Fitch has warned of higher bank risks under the BSP's capital plan.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi closed at about 5,307, down 3.1 percent, or roughly 170 points, its weakest close since 2020 and its worst single session since March of that year. The selling was led by International Container Terminal Services, after a threat to its port licences triggered a rout in the index's heavyweights. The peso weakened 0.25 percent to 63.74 to the dollar, touching 63.81 intraday; UBS now sees it sliding toward 66 by 2027. Why it matters: a weak peso plus a six-year-low equity market makes imported inflation and foreign outflows a two-sided problem. On the data, the balance of payments deficit hit 1.5 billion dollars and gross reserves fell to 98 billion dollars, which the BSP calls adequate. Elsewhere, President Marcos returned from Shenzhen with 41.1 billion pesos in investment pledges that could create over 7,000 jobs, Manila's new free trade pact with Chile covers all current Philippine exports, and diesel and gasoline price rollbacks are expected next week.

Turning to India. Indian equities defied the global rout. The Nifty 50 closed at about 25,801, up 1.1 percent, and the BSE Sensex at roughly 83,920, up 1.1 percent, a gain of 898 points. The rupee strengthened 0.18 percent to 93.29 to the dollar, breaking past 93 for the first time since April, helped by more than 12 billion dollars of foreign buying in Indian equities this month and hedge funds unwinding bearish bets. Why it matters: India is being treated as the safe haven of the AI sell-off, but the currency cuts both ways. One report says the sharp rupee appreciation could cut Indian IT firms' earnings by up to 13 percent in the coming financial year, especially those with heavy European exposure. From the Reserve Bank, forex reserves fell to a three-month low of 705 billion dollars, and the Governor said there is no proposal to end the FCNR deposit incentive scheme early. Meanwhile SEBI is moving to cap the tenure of exchange chief executives and independent directors, and plans a consultation paper to strip away foreign investor compliance constraints that exist nowhere else.

Now to the world. Poland says a Russian missile struck its territory while Ukraine's allies were holding a military exercise. Nobody was hurt and no damage reported, but Prime Minister Tusk called it a provocation from Moscow. Separately, Russia launched one of its biggest air attacks yet on Kyiv's river crossings, days after Ukraine struck the Crimean Bridge, and rejected the idea of security guarantees for Ukraine. Denmark has expelled a Russian diplomat over an alleged plot to assassinate a Ukrainian rapper using a banned local gang. Why it matters: NATO-Russia escalation risk is rising just as markets lose their appetite for risk.

In Washington, President Trump threatened to block US companies from building AI data centres overseas, floating what he called a tremendous tax, with little detail and his own team apparently hearing it for the first time. He also called on Nvidia to reinstate Jensen Huang, who will now testify publicly before the Senate banking panel. Treasury Secretary Bessent says chip export policy to China is unchanged, and TSMC is tightening China AI chip curbs as new US rules close a manufacturing loophole. Why it matters: policy risk is now a core part of the AI valuation problem.

Trump returned from the APEC meetings in Shenzhen, and his trilateral with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, with few concrete outcomes; trade, Taiwan and tech all remain unresolved. He also claimed the Philippines will buy 100 percent of its farm products and energy from the United States.

In the Middle East, the Saudi-led coalition says it intercepted three Houthi missiles, while Iran's Revolutionary Guard claims it downed a US MQ-9 drone over the Strait of Hormuz. Trump says there is no rush on an Iran decision and is looking to next year. Why it matters: Hormuz is the key chokepoint for oil shipments to Asia.

Briefly: COP31 talks have stalled with nations unable to agree a climate plan. Britain and the EU agreed a deal covering food exports, emissions trading and a youth scheme, though analysts say it won't lower food prices. Gen Z protests swept 25 European countries over living costs, military spending and climate. And Grand Theft Auto 6 took 3.8 billion dollars in sales on day one, the biggest entertainment launch in history.

That's your Morning Briefing. Have a good weekend.
