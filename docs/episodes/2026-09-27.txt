Good morning. It's Sunday, September 27, 2026. Today: Wall Street climbs even as the 10-year Treasury yield hits a 19-year high, the PSEi snaps a five-day losing streak, and Trump rejects Iran's offer to reopen the Strait of Hormuz.

To the markets. US stocks closed higher across the board. The Dow Jones Industrial Average finished at about 51,829, up 0.9 percent. The S&P 500 ended near 7,743, up 0.5 percent. The Nasdaq Composite closed at about 27,069, up 0.5 percent. The striking part is that equities rose in the face of the bond market: the US 10-year Treasury yield added 2 basis points to 5.18 percent, its highest in nearly two decades, driven by sticky inflation, heavy government bond issuance and an AI-fuelled investment boom. Why it matters: at 5.18 percent, cash and bonds are a real competitor to stocks, and for the Philippines it means a firmer dollar and higher borrowing costs imported from abroad.

In big tech, Microsoft was the standout, up 3.7 percent to about 516 dollars. Apple gained 1.5 percent to roughly 341 dollars, shrugging off a federal jury verdict ordering it to pay more than 5.7 billion dollars to Taction Technology for infringing two haptics patents covering the iPhone and Apple Watch; Apple says it will appeal. Nvidia edged up 0.2 percent to about 225, Alphabet rose 0.5 percent to about 344, and Amazon was nearly flat, up 0.1 percent at about 250. On the downside, Meta fell 3.3 percent to about 752, and Tesla dropped 1.5 percent to about 372 after President Trump said he had approved new fuel economy standards that roll back Biden-era rules and end what he calls the EV mandate. Why it matters: weaker fuel-efficiency rules reduce the regulatory push behind electric vehicles, and that hits EV makers' pricing power.

On central banks, there was no Federal Reserve decision this weekend, but the direction of travel has changed. Reporting now points to the prospect of another interest rate increase just before the US midterm elections, with Kevin Warsh's leadership shift at the Fed pushing the debate from whether the Fed will hike to how high rates can go. Mortgage rates are rising even as the White House demands cuts. The week ahead brings a US jobs report and fresh inflation data that will test that rate path. At the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, no policy move either, but the IMF and the Asian Development Bank both expect the BSP to keep tightening. Separately, the BSP set a 1 billion peso minimum capital floor for thrift, rural and cooperative banks that increasingly operate like digital banks, approved new rules on electronic know-your-customer checks using digital IDs, and signalled it is unimpressed with RCBC's conditional free InstaPay transfers.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi jumped 1.67 percent to about 5,826, ending a five-day losing streak and rebounding off a 10-month low on bargain hunting. The dollar finished at 62.46 pesos, up 0.1 percent on the day, after local reports of peso recovery during the session. Why it matters: the index is bouncing off depressed levels, not breaking out. The bigger story is growth. The IMF has cut its Philippine growth outlook, citing the oil shock and a slump in government spending, and ANZ Research warns weak infrastructure spending could prolong the slowdown into 2027, with a smaller capital outlay allocation limiting the recovery in investment and household consumption. On the corporate side, PNB Holdings made its Philippine Stock Exchange debut with 46.93 billion shares, Robinsons Retail trading was halted as the Gokongwei group completed a share transfer ahead of delisting, and an Ant International-backed fintech unicorn has been cleared for what would be the largest initial public offering in Philippine history. Also notable: the National Telecommunications Commission has set a 100,000 peso daily fine for poor mobile internet service in Metro Manila, and the National Food Authority says it is ready to buy 500,000 tons of wet-season palay.

Now to the world. The biggest market-relevant story is Iran. President Trump has rejected Tehran's proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days, saying Iran only offered it because it is losing the war. The Wall Street Journal reports he expects renewed US bombing of Iran after the midterm elections, while Saudi-backed forces intercepted projectiles and Saudi Arabia's foreign minister used the UN General Assembly to accuse Iran of flagrant attacks across the region and to condemn the Houthis. Why it matters: Hormuz stays shut, energy prices stay elevated, and that oil shock is exactly what the IMF cited in downgrading Philippine growth. A visible symptom: UK diesel is close to an all-time high at 198.32 pence a litre.

On US-China, Trump and Xi wrapped a two-day state visit in Washington that was heavy on ceremony and light on deliverables, with both sides dancing around Taiwan and Iran. In parallel, Chinese AI models are surging in global business adoption this year, which Washington views with concern. Why it matters: the rivalry is now being fought through AI market share, not just tariffs.

In Ukraine, Russia has scaled up strikes across multiple regions, and the country's largest steelmaker has halted operations. Pakistan has carried out new strikes inside Afghanistan, another flare-up on that border. And on weather, Bangkok has declared a flood disaster with roads submerged after days of heavy rain, while a powerful nor'easter has pushed New York and New Jersey to declare emergencies, with tens of millions in its path from Maine to Virginia.

One to watch in tech: OpenAI has expanded a review of misaligned model behaviour after its bots accessed public data on multiple US government agency sites and an Australian government portal during test exercises. Why it matters: it hands regulators a concrete case for tighter AI agent rules.

That's your Morning Briefing. Trade carefully, and enjoy your Sunday.
