Good morning. It's Wednesday, September 30, 2026. Here's your Morning Briefing on Finance and Geopolitics: US stocks slipped again as Treasury yields climbed, the PSEi hit a 10-month low, and oil and Iran sanctions dominate the geopolitical file.

To the markets. Wall Street closed lower across the board, but only modestly. The Dow Jones ended at about 51,350, down 0.3 percent. The S&P 500 finished near 7,671, down 0.2 percent. The Nasdaq Composite was the most resilient, closing around 26,798, off just 0.1 percent. The pressure came from the bond market: the US 10-year Treasury yield rose 2 basis points to 5.26 percent. Why it matters: with the 10-year above 5 percent, equity valuations and emerging-market currencies, including the peso, stay under strain.

In big tech, Meta was the standout, up 3.2 percent to about 738.79 after launching Muse for Small Business, an AI product aimed at the 200 million small businesses already on its platform. Apple was the worst performer, down 2.7 percent to roughly 329.40. Nvidia slipped 0.7 percent to about 227.21 even as reports highlighted a record buyback, with CEO Jensen Huang arguing the stock is cheap against earnings expectations. Tesla fell 1.3 percent, Alphabet lost 0.5 percent, Microsoft was essentially flat at about 508.96, and Amazon edged up 0.2 percent.

On central banks, the Fed story is a genuine split. New York Fed President John Williams said there's no urgency for the next rate hike, while the dollar hit a two-month high on growing bets for an October increase, and markets are positioning for two more hikes in 2026. The Fed's preferred inflation gauge is due today, expected to show persistent price pressures alongside still-solid consumer spending. Against that, the Conference Board's Consumer Confidence Index tumbled 6.7 points to 81.9, the weakest since 2014 and far below the 89 forecast. Why it matters: confidence is cracking while inflation isn't, which is the hardest combination for the Fed to answer.

For the Bangko Sentral, no new rate decision, but BusinessWorld reports the BSP is expected to tighten further with inflation staying above target this year. Separately, total financial system resources rose nearly 10 percent to 38.09 trillion pesos in July.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi fell 0.9 percent to about 5,738, a 10-month low, extending its slide on rising US yields and climbing world oil prices. The peso weakened 0.11 percent to 62.45 to the dollar. UOB warned the peso could slide toward 66 if dollar strength persists. Why it matters: a weaker peso feeds straight into imported fuel and food costs, which is exactly what the BSP is fighting.

Fuel is already a political issue. Thousands of drivers went on strike nationwide over rising pump prices. The World Bank, meanwhile, urged Manila to accelerate fiscal reforms. In corporate news, Robinsons Retail shares were suspended as the Gokongwei group completed a share transfer ahead of delisting, the SEC opened the market to structured warrants for the first time, and Alternergy raised 2 billion pesos in its debut fixed-rate note issue. Elsewhere, former House Speaker Martin Romualdez is seeking bail in a 7-billion-peso case, Albay declared a state of calamity over dengue, and Air France resumes nonstop Manila–Paris flights in October.

Now to the world. Oil is the market's main geopolitical channel right now. Washington sanctioned 10 entities and individuals across China, Pakistan, Turkiye, Iran and Saudi Arabia for allegedly supporting Iran's military, part of an escalating pressure campaign. Britain is also investigating what police describe as a potential Iranian bomb plot targeting RAF Fairford, a British base used by US forces. Why it matters: every notch of Iran escalation puts a risk premium into crude, and the Philippines imports nearly all of its fuel. Separately, a threatened US diesel export ban is being floated to shield American consumers from rising costs; if it happened, it would reroute global diesel flows and lift prices elsewhere.

On trade, America's new import restrictions on Canadian goods came into force. President Trump says he expects Ottawa to concede within weeks; Canadian officials insist they won't sign a bad deal. JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon used the moment to push for what he calls a big, beautiful trade deal between the US and Europe, arguing current disputes are minor next to the potential gains. And Ford CEO Jim Farley warned it's already too late for Europe to fend off Chinese automakers, though not yet for the US.

In the Middle East, Israeli settlers attacked a West Bank village, burning two homes belonging to the al-Tubasi family and clashing with Israeli security forces. Qatar's prime minister accused Prime Minister Netanyahu of war crimes and of targeting Doha to deflect from Gaza. Israel's use of white phosphorus in Lebanon is also under renewed scrutiny.

In Europe, more than 400 people have been detained as French student protests over education cuts and school conditions spread nationwide. Spain announced a ban on evictions after mass protests over the removal of an 87-year-old woman from her Madrid flat; the real estate firm backed down and restored her old rent, and the measures must clear parliament within 30 days. Estonia blamed Russia for an arson attack on a defence company supplying Ukraine, the latest in a string of alleged sabotage across NATO states. And Morocco named its first female prime minister, only the second woman ever to lead an Arab government.

Two quick tech and policy notes. President Trump met AI executives and says they signed what he called a morally binding AI agreement, while House Speaker Mike Johnson said he hopes guardrails stay voluntary. And smart ring maker Oura pulled its 15 billion dollar US listing days after announcing it, citing market uncertainty. Why it matters: that's a live signal that the IPO window is tightening as yields rise.

That's your briefing for Wednesday, September 30. Watch the US inflation print today, and trade safely.
