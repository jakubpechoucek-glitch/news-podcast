Good morning. It's Monday, November 23, 2026. Today: a brutal AI-led selloff on Wall Street, a rebound in Philippine and Indian stocks, Asian governments stepping in to steady markets, and no deal at COP31.

Start with Wall Street, where the latest close was ugly. The Dow Jones Industrial Average finished at about 48,147, down 1.9 percent. The S&P 500 closed at about 6,910, down 3.7 percent. The Nasdaq Composite dropped 5.3 percent to about 22,618, capping tech's worst week on Wall Street since 2020. Money ran into government bonds: the US 10-year Treasury yield fell 14 basis points to 4.91 percent.

The damage was concentrated in the AI complex. Nvidia was the epicentre, down 14.0 percent to about 125.53 dollars, closing out its longest weekly losing streak since 2008. Tesla fell 7.6 percent to about 345.82. Meta lost 5.9 percent to about 560.84. Amazon fell 4.1 percent to about 246.82, Microsoft 2.8 percent to about 484.52, Alphabet 2.7 percent to about 265.29, and Apple held up best, down 1.9 percent to about 342.56. The proximate cause is sentiment rather than results: mounting fears the AI build-out is overheated, underlined by Anthropic's record Nasdaq debut under the ticker ANT landing right in the middle of the rout. Why it matters: when the biggest index weights fall this hard, passive funds everywhere, including Philippine-held global funds, take the hit.

On central banks, the Federal Reserve has already raised rates for the first time since 2023, and Fed speakers have been out in force. Markets now treat another hike next month as close to certain; the open question is the size. Rate-hike bets eased slightly over the weekend, which helped gold rebound 1.1 percent to about 3,575 dollars an ounce after a 14-month low, and nudged the dollar off a 19-month high. Why it matters: a Fed still tightening into a falling stock market is the single biggest variable for Asian currencies and bond yields this quarter. There was no fresh Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas rate decision over the weekend, but there was policy news: the BSP is proposing to open the retail payment system to more fintech and qualified non-bank players, with licensing and security conditions, and stricter rules for third-party payment initiation services. It also signed a cooperation pact with Bank Negara Malaysia covering cross-border payments, and warned the public about scammers impersonating BSP officials.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi closed at about 5,364, up 1.1 percent, a bounce after the index plunged 4 percent on Friday to 5,257, a six-and-a-half-year low. Brokers see it testing 5,400, but most local commentary expects continued weakness on global and domestic growth worries. The peso was slightly softer at 63.83 to the dollar, up 0.14 percent. Why it matters: a weak peso plus a hiking Fed keeps imported inflation and local borrowing costs elevated. In corporate news, Robinsons Land is injecting its biggest mall into RL Commercial REIT through a 20.16-billion-peso property-for-share swap, a sizeable test of REIT appetite in a falling market. Department of Finance Undersecretary Ma. Angela Ignacio is being eyed to head the Maharlika Investment Corporation. Politically, Senator Panfilo Lacson is challenging President Marcos's explanation that the Iran war caused the economic slowdown, asking why Philippine growth fell further than the rest of ASEAN. And watch local rates: dealers say T-bill and bond yields may rise this week as a retail dollar bond offering drains peso liquidity.

Turning to India. Indian equities went the other way. The Nifty 50 closed at about 25,801, up 1.1 percent, and the BSE Sensex at about 83,920, up 1.1 percent, nine-month highs and a fourth straight weekly gain, driven by strong earnings and banking stocks. The rupee strengthened, with the dollar down 0.68 percent to 92.66 rupees. GIFT Nifty futures point to a gap-up open today, with 26,000 in view, though rising crude and West Asia tensions are a drag. On the Reserve Bank of India, inflation has breached target and analysts argue the RBI cannot afford a timid December policy. The RBI says foreign exchange inflows under its swap facility have reached 72.85 billion dollars, and deputy governor Murmu has told bank boards to own their AI and technology risk decisions rather than delegate it. Elsewhere, Indian IT's recovery remains elusive, with better second-quarter growth undercut by weak deal bookings, and industry body CII has published a 15-point reform agenda aimed at financing a 26.5-trillion-dollar economy by 2047. Why it matters: foreign inflows into Indian equities are at records, but analysts warn that is sentiment, not a structural re-rating.

Now to the world and geopolitics. COP31 collapsed without a major agreement after a dispute over whether to mention fossil fuels, making it the longest-running summit in the process's history. Delegates from the Philippines, Kenya and the Marshall Islands expressed dismay. Why it matters: no deal means no new multilateral climate finance commitments for exposed economies like the Philippines.

The Iran conflict is still the main oil risk. Iran's navy says tankers breaching its rules were set ablaze in the Strait of Hormuz, and the Revolutionary Guard warned of enemies stoking domestic unrest. Saudi Arabia says it destroyed a Houthi drone, and Bahrain says an industrial facility was hit by Iranian projectiles, while Tehran says it remains open to a deal with Washington. Despite that, Brent crude slipped 0.8 percent to about 91.80 dollars a barrel, with traders now focused on this week's OPEC-plus output decision amid supply-glut concerns.

On Russia and Ukraine, a Russia-bound ship was towed away after a drone strike in the Gulf of Finland, which Finnish defence forces attribute to Ukraine, and a Russian ship was hit by a suspected drone at a Libyan port. Poland says Russia twice tried to assassinate a Ukrainian rapper, echoing a Danish plot allegation. Why it matters: the war is now routinely touching shipping and European soil, a persistent insurance and freight cost.

In Asia, governments are intervening. China will cut margin requirements from November 30, freeing up roughly 400 billion yuan, and state funds bought a record amount of ETFs on Friday. South Korea is activating market stabilisation funds after the KOSPI hit a ten-month low, falling nearly 3 percent on the tech selloff. Why it matters: official buying can slow a rout but rarely ends one.

Finally, in Washington, Democrats have won at least 258 House seats to the Republicans' 173, the largest House majority since 1992, and President Trump says he will work with Democrats to cap credit card interest rates at 10 percent.

That's your Morning Briefing for Monday, November 23. Trade carefully.
