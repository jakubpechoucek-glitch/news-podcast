Good morning. It's Saturday, October 10, 2026, and here's your Morning Briefing on finance and geopolitics. Today: Wall Street closes the week higher even as yields climb, the PSEi finally snaps a losing streak, and Washington strikes an energy deal with Moscow.

To markets. Wall Street finished Friday firmly in the green. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at about 51,655, up 0.8 percent. The S&P 500 ended near 7,812, up 0.6 percent, and the Nasdaq Composite at roughly 27,366, up 0.6 percent. All three posted weekly gains, helped by a retreat in oil prices and optimism ahead of third-quarter earnings from the big banks, including Wells Fargo and Goldman Sachs. Why it matters: the rally is narrow and leaning hard on artificial intelligence names, and the bull market is approaching its fourth anniversary with margin debt at record levels, which amplifies losses if sentiment turns.

Bonds went the other way. The US 10-year Treasury yield rose 1 basis point to 5.24 percent, with Treasuries slipping on inflation worries as energy costs stay elevated. Reuters flagged four signs the bond market could get uglier from here. Why it matters: a 5-plus percent 10-year is the ceiling on every risk asset, including Philippine equities.

Among the big tech names, Amazon led with a 3.3 percent gain, Microsoft rose 2.4 percent, and Tesla added 2.1 percent even as it dropped the "Full Self-Driving" brand name in Europe after German regulators called it misleading. Alphabet gained 1 percent. On the downside, Meta slipped 0.3 percent, Nvidia fell 0.5 percent, and Apple dropped 1.1 percent. The real carnage was in telecoms: Verizon posted its worst day since 2002, with AT&T and T-Mobile down as much as 11 percent after SpaceX bought low-band spectrum from Grain Management to push Starlink into mobile service. Delta also cut its 2026 profit outlook despite strong demand, after fuel costs added 6 billion dollars to annual expenses.

On central banks, no Federal Reserve decision this week, but the signal is clear: officials are expected to take their time with further rate hikes, and markets are pricing in more tightening, not easing. Separately, President Trump has appointed a committee to investigate statements by Fed Governor Lisa Cook, a fresh attempt to remove her after the Supreme Court blocked the first. Why it matters: Fed independence is now a live market risk, not a talking point. In Manila, no Bangko Sentral rate move this week either, but Bank of America expects more BSP hikes ahead as inflation stays elevated. The BSP says confidence in the banking system is intact and has told the Development Bank of the Philippines to show strong justification for extending regulatory relief.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi jumped 1.6 percent to close at about 5,701, reclaiming the 5,700 level as bargain hunters returned and global oil prices eased. That snaps a four-week losing streak and lifts the index off a six-year low, though analysts warn the risks have not gone away. The peso firmed 0.25 percent to 62.72 to the dollar.

Three stories matter. First, the government has declared a national energy emergency as Asia faces an energy crunch tied to the Iran war, and the Philippines is helping advance the Asia Zero Emission Community's regional oil stockpiling push. Why it matters: a Hormuz-dependent economy has almost no buffer if shipping is disrupted. Second, gross international reserves have hit a three-year low, and growth is now seen below 3 percent on weak demand and inflation. Why it matters: thinner reserves and slower growth limit how hard the BSP can defend the peso. Third, Metro Pacific Investments has completed a 12.4-billion-peso deal with the San Miguel group, taking it to nearly half of Meralco. And a sobering data point: Filipinos lost 121 billion pesos to scams in 2025.

Turning to India. The latest closes show the Nifty 50 at about 22,232, down 1.6 percent, and the BSE Sensex at roughly 71,593, down 1.4 percent, giving back an earlier rebound that had been led by IT and banking stocks on easing crude. The rupee was little changed at 96.78 to the dollar, recovering intraday to 96.73 after heavy Reserve Bank intervention, but still posting a weekly fall. It is down about 7 percent year to date, with persistent corporate dollar demand and shrinking reserves.

The Reserve Bank has raised the repo rate to 5.50 percent and shifted its stance to calibrated tightening. It is also lifting the daily cash reserve ratio maintenance requirement to 99 percent from October 16, and fears of open market bond sales have pushed the 10-year government security to a three-year high. Why it matters: India is tightening into a slowdown in parts of its economy while the Financial Times calls it an economy of two speeds. On growth, UNCTAD still sees India as the fastest-growing large economy at 7.3 percent in 2026, and the World Bank has lifted its outlook to 7.1 percent. Politically, New Delhi has called US Vice President JD Vance's comments about Indian immigrants "deeply offensive."

To the world and geopolitics. The biggest story: President Trump has struck a deal with Vladimir Putin to supply Russian diesel to the United States and global markets. President Zelensky called it a "gift to Putin" that will prolong the war. Why it matters: it is an explicit trade of sanctions pressure for lower pump prices ahead of the US midterms, and traders on Kalshi still expect petrol above 4 dollars a gallon on November 3.

Energy supply is tightening from two other directions. Tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz have surged to a wartime high as Iran tries to choke off oil exports, and Hurricane Isaias has shut in almost three-quarters of US Gulf of Mexico oil production while threatening refineries. Why it matters: refining capacity, not crude, is the binding constraint, and that keeps diesel and jet fuel costs high from Manila to Mumbai.

Elsewhere, the United States has imposed sanctions on the International Criminal Court, a move the court calls an assault on the rule of law, with member states including the United Kingdom saying they strongly disagree. A magnitude 7.7 earthquake has struck Panama, damaging buildings and triggering tsunami warnings for neighbouring countries. The UN envoy warns Yemen has returned to full-scale war, with Saudi Arabia pressing the Security Council to act. And Nobel laureate Paul Krugman says France may have become "too big to save," warning of a looming ugly crisis as former ECB chief Jean-Claude Trichet urges political compromise. Finally, South African jurist Navi Pillay, the former UN human rights chief, has won the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize.

That's your Morning Briefing. Have a good weekend, and we'll see you Monday.
