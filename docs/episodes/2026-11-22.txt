Good morning. It's Sunday, November 22, 2026. Today: Wall Street's worst tech week since 2020, the PSEi at a six-year low, and Iranian missiles landing in Bahrain.

To markets. Friday's close capped a brutal week. The Dow Jones Industrial Average finished at about 48,147, down 1.9 percent. The S&P 500 closed near 6,910, down 3.7 percent. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.3 percent to roughly 22,618. Both the S&P and the Dow are now in correction territory. Why it matters: the AI trade that carried this market all year is unwinding, fast.

Nvidia was the epicentre, plunging 14.0 percent to about 125.53 and completing its longest weekly losing streak since the 2008 financial crisis. Once the world's most valuable company, it now ranks fourth, behind Apple, Microsoft and Alphabet. Former chief executive Jensen Huang will testify publicly before the Senate banking committee following criticism over his links to a suspected Chinese spy. Across the rest of big tech: Tesla fell 7.6 percent to about 345.82, Meta 5.9 percent to roughly 560.84, Amazon 4.1 percent to about 246.82, Microsoft 2.8 percent to around 484.52, Alphabet 2.7 percent to about 265.29, and Apple held up best, down 1.9 percent to roughly 342.56. Anthropic's record 81 billion dollar Nasdaq debut landed in the middle of all this, and its shares slid — a warning sign for a market now absorbing new share supply as companies shift from buybacks to issuance.

Money ran to safety. The US 10-year Treasury yield fell 14 basis points to 4.91 percent, back below 5 percent for the first time in months. Bitcoin broke below 60,000 dollars, a two-year low, and prediction market Kalshi now puts the odds of the S&P 500 reaching 8,000 next year at extremely low, down from above 70 percent.

Central banks. No fresh Federal Reserve decision, but the signalling is hawkish. Fed Governor Cook said rate hikes are needed to bring high inflation down, and Barron's reports the Fed is expected to raise rates next month, with the size still an open question. President Trump says rates are too high and the Fed is politicised. Chairman Warsh has asked officials to speak less; instead they are speaking more. Why it matters: a hiking Fed alongside a deflating AI trade is the worst possible mix for risk assets. From the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, no policy move this weekend.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi closed at about 5,307, down 3.1 percent, a six-year low, with the selloff led by International Container Terminal Services after a threat to its port licence. The peso weakened slightly to 63.80 to the dollar. Why it matters: a domestic regulatory shock is compounding the global risk-off move, and foreign money is leaving.

The balance of payments is also deteriorating. The 10-month deficit of 1.5 billion dollars has already exceeded the full-year 2026 target, with gross reserves down to 98 billion dollars. On the positive side, President Marcos returned from China with 41 billion dollars in investment pledges, and Manila is targeting 1 billion pesos in Canadian investment under the new free trade agreement, aiming to make Canada a top-10 investment source within three years.

Also on the policy desk: the Department of Finance is pushing for swift passage of the global minimum tax bill, having consulted Southeast Asian counterparts on fiscal incentive adjustments. And the BSP signed a pact with Bank Negara Malaysia on digital payments and Islamic finance. On the ground, a powerful earthquake shook the Philippines, and Japan has agreed to supply coastal radar and drones for maritime surveillance.

Turning to India, where the story is the mirror image. The Nifty 50 rose 1.1 percent to about 25,801, a nine-month high, and the Sensex gained 1.1 percent to roughly 83,920. Over the week the Nifty added 2.6 percent, or 656 points, on record foreign institutional inflows. The rupee strengthened 0.7 percent to 92.67 to the dollar. Why it matters: India is being treated as the haven inside emerging markets while the AI trade unwinds.

Banking led, with the Nifty Bank index up around 16 percent month-to-date to a record high, on healthy credit demand. Healthcare also ran hard, Max Healthcare up more than 14 percent on the week. Shriram Finance replaces Trent in the Sensex from December 21.

At the Reserve Bank of India, foreign exchange reserves fell by 7.7 billion dollars to 690 billion. The Governor said global growth should be marginally higher in 2026, supported by technology-related investment. Separately, India and Canada concluded a sixth round of trade talks, and ratings agency Crisil flagged inflation and AI disruption as the new risks to an otherwise resilient growth story. One more number: gold in India closed at a 14-month low of about 121,949 rupees per 10 grams, with the international price at 3,539 dollars an ounce.

Now to the world. The biggest escalation is in the Gulf. Iran's Revolutionary Guard struck US military positions at two bases in Bahrain, at al-Riffa and Awali, with Bahrain reporting a fire at an industrial facility and no injuries. Houthi strikes continue, keeping Saudi Arabia on edge, and Iran's president described the pressure on his country as a silent economic war. Why it matters: direct Iranian fire on a Gulf state hosting US forces is the clearest escalation yet, and it puts a floor under oil prices even as analysts forecast crude normalising near 70 dollars a barrel by end-2027 on surplus supply.

Second, Russia and Ukraine. President Zelensky called strikes on the Crimean Bridge a fair response after 109 separate Russian aerial attacks on Ukrainian bridges. Finland's defence forces say Ukraine was likely behind a drone attack on a Russia-bound vessel in the Gulf of Finland, and Denmark has expelled Russia's defence attaché over an alleged plot to assassinate a US-Ukrainian rapper. Why it matters: the war is spilling into NATO's Baltic waters and into European capitals.

Third, trade and technology policy. President Trump, speaking after a historic trilateral summit in China with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, threatened to stop US companies building AI data centres abroad, warning of a tremendous tax. Details were thin, and Treasury Secretary Bessent said Nvidia's chip export policy is unchanged. Why it matters: it adds policy risk to an AI sector already in a selloff.

And briefly: COP31 climate talks are deadlocked over fossil fuels with nearly 200 countries unable to agree. The UK and EU agreed a deal covering food exports, emissions trading and a youth scheme, though analysts say it won't lower food prices. France saw a sixth straight weekend of protests with police dispersing crowds. The FBI is investigating an apparent hack of its National Crime Information Center database, the second recent intrusion. And on a lighter note, Grand Theft Auto 6 took 3.8 billion dollars in a single day, the biggest entertainment launch in history.

That's your Morning Briefing for Sunday, November 22. Trade carefully out there.
