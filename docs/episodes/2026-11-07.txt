Good morning. It's Saturday, November 7, 2026. Here's your tight read on markets, the Philippines, India and the world — the facts, and why each one matters.

Start with Wall Street, where a split tape tells the story. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at about 51,286, down 0.5 percent. The S&P 500 finished near 7,377, up 0.3 percent. The Nasdaq Composite rose 1.1 percent to about 24,579 — a bounce, but one that still left the index in correction territory and down for a fourth straight week. Why it matters: the AI trade is being repriced, not abandoned. This week's rout, which wiped trillions off technology valuations, was driven by fears that the Democratic midterm sweep means tighter regulation of AI and data centres. Leading Democrats are now explicitly calling for a national data centre moratorium.

The single names split along exactly that line. Nvidia rebounded 2.8 percent to about 186.64, leading a chip recovery. Tesla jumped 4.2 percent to about 358.22 after Elon Musk said Cybertruck production in Texas will wind down next year to make room for the driverless Cybercab. Alphabet added 0.8 percent. On the losing side, Meta dropped 4.0 percent to about 605.08, Microsoft fell 1.0 percent after saying it will cut the number of so-called neocloud partners it uses as the AI buildout shows strain, Apple slipped 0.5 percent and Amazon 0.4 percent. Watch the AI debt market too: CoreWeave's bond yield has topped 16 percent, and investors now expect Anthropic to list below the $1.5 trillion valuation once floated.

Bonds and central banks. The US 10-year Treasury yield rose 2 basis points to 4.86 percent, after October non-farm payrolls came in at 165,000, nearly triple the 59,000 expected, and unemployment slipped to 4.1 percent. Why it matters: that is a hike signal, not a cut signal. San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly said Americans are on a treadmill of high inflation and more rate hikes are likely; Richmond's Tom Barkin sees hikes ahead at a gradual pace; and John Williams repeated that inflation is too high against the 2 percent target. Traders raised bets on a December increase, and gold fell on the data. No fresh policy decision from the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, but Deutsche Bank warned the BSP against another jumbo hike while raising its 2026 Philippine inflation forecast to 5.9 percent.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi slipped 0.3 percent to close at 5,761.95 on profit-taking and macro headwinds. The peso finished at 62.75 to the dollar, firmer by 0.9 percent on the day, though local desks flagged it drifting back toward the 63 level through the week. The data was mixed to soft: unemployment inched up to 5.4 percent in September, third-quarter agricultural output fell 5 percent led by crops and fisheries, and economists polled expect third-quarter GDP growth of just 2.8 percent. S&P Global Ratings went further, saying the Philippines is already in stagflation as surging energy costs and weak government spending bite. Why it matters: that combination makes the BSP's December call genuinely contested — economists see inflation staying below 8 percent, but are split on whether another hike is needed. One relief valve: the Inquirer reports US midterm results could cut local fuel prices by as much as 13.50 pesos a litre. On corporates, DMCI Holdings lifted nine-month profit 28 percent to 15 billion pesos. Politically, the impeachment court admitted Vice President Sara Duterte's financial records and restored its gag rule.

Turning to India. Indian equities had a blowout session. The Sensex surged 1,718 points, or 2.2 percent, to about 81,028, and the Nifty 50 rose 2.0 percent to about 25,030, reclaiming the 25,000 level. Both indices posted their biggest weekly gains in six years — notably, in a week when global technology stocks were being hammered. The rupee strengthened past 94, closing at 94.46 to the dollar. Indian government bonds had their best week in seven years. Why it matters: the driver is oil. Crude fell sharply after the US midterms, and India, as a major importer, is the clearest beneficiary, pulling foreign money back into both bonds and equities. From the Reserve Bank of India, a record dividend of 2.87 lakh crore rupees to the central government for the last fiscal year, which eases New Delhi's fiscal maths. The RBI also met bankers over rising AI-driven cyberattacks and has mandated security audits. NBFC credit growth picked up to 15.4 percent in September, and the Finance Ministry projects 7.3 percent growth in the second quarter of this fiscal year.

To the world and geopolitics. First, Iran. Qatar's prime minister urged Washington and Tehran not to wait for the perfect moment to reach a deal ending the war. Why it matters: a resolution is one of the few forces that could pull oil lower still and take pressure off Philippine fuel and Indian inflation — and the conflict is already visible in corporate results, with British Airways parent IAG reporting a more than 12 percent drop in third-quarter net profit on higher fuel costs and conflict-related cancellations.

Second, the US midterms aftershocks. The Democratic sweep has upended Congress, though foreign policy still sits largely with President Trump. Inside the Republican camp, Marjorie Taylor Greene warned the Miller-Wiles White House personnel shakeup will sink the party's 2028 chances. Why it matters: the policy risk markets are now pricing is domestic regulation of AI and data centres, plus a different fiscal path into the Fed's next decisions.

Third, aviation security in Europe. A new wave of hoax bomb threats hit airlines across London, Dublin and Helsinki this week, disrupting dozens of flights. No explosives were found, and a Russian extremist group has denied responsibility. Why it matters: repeated disruption raises airline costs just as fuel bills are already squeezing margins.

Fourth, Paraguay. President Santiago Peña is under arrest and Pedro Alliana has been sworn in as president, amid the collapse of the popular lender Ueno Bank. Why it matters: a bank failure triggering a leadership change is a reminder that emerging-market credit stress can escalate fast.

Three quick ones. The European Commission has told TikTok to fix what it calls a harmful For You feed, threatening fines over algorithmic rabbit holes. Washington is pressing Beijing to honour its commitments under the Trump-Xi Busan trade deal on tariffs and tech restrictions. And copper fell for a fourth straight session, heading for its worst week since March, dragged down by the AI selloff — a useful signal that this is being read as an industrial capex story, not just a tech story.

Finally, New Zealanders voted in a tightly contested election, and Pope Leo XIV has arrived in Uruguay to begin a 12-day South American tour, weeks after lung surgery.

That's your Morning Briefing for Saturday, November 7. Have a good weekend.
