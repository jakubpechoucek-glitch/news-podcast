# Morning Briefing: Finance & Geopolitics
**Tuesday, September 15th, 2026**

Good morning, and welcome to the Morning Briefing. I'm your host, and today is Tuesday, September 15th. We've got several interconnected stories shaping markets and global stability, so let's dig in.

We're starting with a developing crisis in the Middle East that's already rippling through global energy and financial markets. Over the last 36 hours, there's been a significant escalation in the Strait of Hormuz. A tanker called the El Gaia has been attacked, and we're seeing conflicting accounts of what happened. Iran says the vessel hit mines, but the United States military says the ship was struck first by a missile and then by a drone. Two sailors are missing. This matters because the Strait of Hormuz is one of the world's most critical chokepoints for oil transit. When tensions spike there, oil prices spike with them.

That's directly connected to what we're seeing in global financial markets right now. The US ten-year Treasury yield has climbed to 5.04 percent, the highest level since 2007. That's a significant move. Why? Well, traders are pricing in multiple factors. First, there's inflation pressure from higher oil prices, which climbed above 108 dollars a barrel partly due to the Middle East tensions we just mentioned. Second, investors are now betting the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates later this week. Higher borrowing costs across the board ripple everywhere—mortgages, car loans, government debt. And for the United States, that's especially acute because the federal government is borrowing at record levels.

Now, this connects to a broader competence issue that came out yesterday. The Pentagon inspector general confirmed that the ongoing Iran war—which has now cost an average of 246 million dollars per day over its first five months—has created munitions shortfalls for the Defense Department. The Congressional Budget Office calculated 38.1 billion dollars spent through August first. Despite President Trump's claims that US supplies are "virtually limitless," the inspector found there's a genuine bottleneck in resupply capacity. This is a real constraint on military operations, and it's also a fiscal constraint. Defense spending that high, without commensurate tax increases, has to be borrowed.

Speaking of which, there's been a broader bond-market selloff globally. In the UK, economists are now urging Chancellor John Healey to pressure the Bank of England to slow down its quantitative tightening—that's the program where the central bank sells off bonds it holds to drain money from the economy. The Bank's bond-selling program has already cost the exchequer billions of pounds, and as borrowing costs rise across the Atlantic, they're rising in London too. This is genuinely contentious because it's blurring the line between monetary policy, which is supposed to be independent, and fiscal policy, which is supposed to be controlled by elected officials.

The UK's also grappling with an aging population. Official wage growth has slowed to 3.9 percent. That's going to trigger an increase in the state pension—likely topping 13,000 pounds a year under the triple-lock mechanism. The government's now said that retirees with no other income won't pay tax on that new pension amount, but this has reignited the generational fairness debate. How do you sustain pensions for aging populations in a lower-growth environment? That's a question every developed economy is wrestling with.

Now let's zoom out to the geopolitical level. We've had another reminder of rising Russia-NATO tensions. Denmark's prime minister has accused Russia of "reckless" behavior after a Russian warship fired flares at a Danish military helicopter. It's a minor incident, but it's part of a pattern of probing and escalation in the Baltic region. Separately, there's been suspected sabotage of Dutch rail infrastructure—objects found on multiple tracks caused major disruptions Tuesday. While there's no confirmed attribution yet, European officials are on high alert for hybrid warfare tactics.

Then there's the space domain, which is becoming an active theater. The US has apparently tested or deployed some kind of weapon in space—the exact nature isn't clear—but this points to a developing arms race in orbit. Russia and China have similar programs. The geopolitical implication is clear: if you can't control space, you can't control global communications, GPS, or military command and control. This is a long-term strategic competition that'll shape the 2030s and beyond.

Shifting to trade and economic blocs. Canada is actively repositioning itself, and it's a sign of how fractured the traditional Western alliance has become. Prime Minister Mark Carney, who's now leading the country, is pitching Canada as a "safe harbour" for investment and is explicitly building what he calls a "unique security and economic alliance" with Europe. This is happening against the backdrop of a bitter trade war with the United States. Carney's launched a massive Canada Investment Summit with over 160 projects on offer—roughly a trillion dollars worth of investment opportunities. He's essentially telling foreign investors: invest in Canada as a base to serve European markets, and avoid US tariff exposure. That's a major realignment.

On the technology front, there's a brewing regulatory battle that'll shape the next decade. AI companies—OpenAI, Google, Anthropic—are discussing collaboration on AI safety, which sounds collaborative until you realize it might be a way to preempt government regulation. Anthropic's co-founder Jack Clark told the BBC that an AI kill switch may need to be mandatory. But President Trump dismissed all AI safety concerns as a "hoax," saying claims about AI "taking over the world" are nonsense. Congress is divided on this. With Trump opposed and Congress fractured, new AI safety legislation is unlikely to pass anytime soon, despite mounting pressure from the tech industry itself. That regulatory vacuum could be dangerous.

There's also been a significant blow to the crypto industry. The US Senate's crypto bill—the Clarity Act—failed cloture on Tuesday, collapsing efforts to establish the first comprehensive federal framework for digital-asset markets. It's a defeat for the industry's lobbying push.

Lastly, one more climate-geopolitical story with economic consequences: the Panama Canal is planning further traffic cuts due to worsening drought from the El Niño phenomenon. That's one of the world's most important shipping lanes, so any reduction there chokes global trade. Combined with the Suez and Strait of Hormuz tensions, you're looking at genuine logistics friction in global supply chains.

To sum up: the Middle East is heating up, energy prices are rising, that's driving inflation fears and bond-market selloffs, the Fed's likely raising rates this week, which cascades into higher borrowing costs everywhere. Meanwhile, traditional alliances are fracturing—Canada's looking to Europe, the space race is militarizing, AI regulation is stalled in Washington, and logistics chokepoints are tightening. It's a complex picture, but the common thread is rising costs—both financial and geopolitical.
