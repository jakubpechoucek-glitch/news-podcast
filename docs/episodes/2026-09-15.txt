Oil prices have surged past 108 dollars a barrel following fresh Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabian infrastructure. The Houthis, backed by Iran, have now made good on threats to target Saudi energy facilities, and Saudi Arabia has vowed a firm response. Here's why this matters to your wallet: oil underpins inflation globally. With crude climbing, inflation expectations are rising in the United States and the UK, which means bond markets are repricing faster than central banks might like. U.S. Treasury yields have now hit five percent for the first time since 2023. The ten-year yield is the highest since 2007. This sell-off in government debt is accelerating because investors believe the Federal Reserve will need to raise interest rates this week, possibly multiple times, to combat the inflation surge driven by energy prices.

British wage growth has slowed to just 3.9 percent, but because oil is hitting the UK supply chains too, that wage growth is being squeezed by renewed cost-of-living pressure. Here's the awkward part: UK state pensions are indexed to wage growth through something called the triple lock. So as wages slow, pension payouts will rise to just above 13,000 pounds a year. This is reigniting a serious debate about generational fairness and whether the state pension system is sustainable long-term. Younger workers already struggling with inflation and borrowing costs will be funding higher pension bills—that's the political math Westminster is wrestling with.

Adding to the UK's economic headwinds, the government is now moving to nationalize Speciality Steel UK, a troubled steelmaker. Production at its plants in South Yorkshire and the West Midlands had paused earlier this year. This isn't just a steel story—it signals the government's willingness to intervene in critical industrial sectors, but it also underscores how fragile supply chains remain in post-crisis Britain.

Warsh's credibility is on the line because his predecessors have struggled with exactly this kind of impossible trinity: inflation they can't easily control, political pressure they can't ignore, and a presidential administration willing to criticize the Fed publicly.

The tension here is real: billionaires and their companies are profiting enormously from AI, but some of them are now publicly warning that the pace of development is reckless.
