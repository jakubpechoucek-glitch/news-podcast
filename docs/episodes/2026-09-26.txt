Good morning. It's Saturday, September 26, 2026. Here's your briefing: US stocks closed the week higher even as the 10-year yield pushed further above 5 percent, the PSEi snapped a five-day losing streak, and Iran floated reopening the Strait of Hormuz.

To markets. Wall Street finished firmly in the green. The Dow closed at about 51,829, up 0.9 percent. The S&P 500 ended near 7,743, up 0.5 percent. The Nasdaq Composite finished around 27,069, up 0.5 percent. But the bond market is telling a different story: the US 10-year Treasury yield rose another 2 basis points to 5.18 percent. Why it matters: yields at these levels raise the cost of everything from US mortgages to emerging-market borrowing, and they cap how far equities can run.

In big tech, Microsoft was the standout, up 3.7 percent to 516.17 after it repackaged its business AI tools into a single Copilot app as it tries to close the gap with Anthropic. Apple rose 1.5 percent to 341.07. Alphabet added 0.5 percent to 343.92, Nvidia was nearly flat, up 0.2 percent to 225.07, and Amazon was up 0.1 percent to 249.67. The two losers: Meta fell 3.3 percent to 751.66, even as options desks flagged it as a potential breakout candidate into 2027, and Tesla dropped 1.5 percent to 372.11, ahead of the opening of its first heavy-duty truck factory in Sparks, Nevada, which will scale production of the Semi.

On central banks, the direction of travel has flipped. Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson said further, in her word, modest rate moves are likely ahead to bring inflation back to target. Fed Governor Michael Barr echoed that more hikes are probably needed. The New York Times reports the Fed is confronting the prospect of another increase just ahead of the US midterms, and Kevin Warsh's restructuring of the institution is meeting internal resistance. Next week's US jobs report and inflation data are the next real test. Why it matters: a Fed still tightening into an election year is the single biggest risk to Asian currencies and to Philippine borrowing costs.

Locally, the Bangko Sentral is in the same camp. BusinessMirror reports the BSP believes it has enough room for more rate hikes. No policy decision this week.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi rebounded 1.7 percent to close at about 5,826, snapping a five-day losing streak and reclaiming the 5,800 level on bargain hunting and foreign inflows. The peso was little changed, with the dollar up 0.1 percent at 62.46 pesos. PNB Holdings made its stock market debut, eyeing redevelopment of prime assets, and PSE president Ramon Monzon is talking up the upcoming GCash IPO as a potential catalyst for the local capital market.

Three things to watch. First, BSP data shows residential property prices posted their slowest annual growth since the series began in 2019, with declines outside Metro Manila offsetting gains in the capital. Why it matters: it's the clearest sign yet that high rates are biting the property sector. Second, the BSP has mandated a phase-out of SMS one-time passwords, pushing major banks and e-wallets toward biometrics. Third, pump relief is coming: diesel may fall by as much as 7 pesos a liter next week. And the ADB has approved a 1.5 billion dollar loan to fund the Philippines' Middle East crisis response, a reminder of how exposed the country is to OFW remittance disruption.

Also on the radar at home: the BPO sector says 45 billion dollars in revenues is within reach by 2027, the USDA sees Philippine rice imports climbing to 5.7 million metric tons next year, and the NFA says it's ready to buy 500,000 tons of wet-season palay.

Now to the world. The biggest market story is energy. Iran has offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days and restart nuclear talks, with its president saying it's up to the United States to choose whether it wants to end the war. That came the same morning the Houthis launched a fresh wave of missile and drone attacks on Saudi targets. Saudi crude exports have nonetheless hit their highest level since the Iran war began, despite a pipeline outage. Why it matters: Hormuz reopening would be the single fastest route to lower global oil and diesel prices, and Philippine pump prices follow within weeks.

Fuel remains the pressure point elsewhere. UK average diesel is at 198.32 pence a liter, close to an all-time high, and energy experts warn a proposed US diesel export ban would push costs higher both at home and abroad. Meanwhile Pakistan's prime minister Shehbaz Sharif told the UN that any Houthi attack on Mecca or Medina would cross a red line, as the army chiefs of Islamabad, Riyadh and Ankara met over closer military ties.

On US-China, Trump and Xi wrapped a two-day state visit that was heavy on ceremony and light on deliverables, dancing around Taiwan and Iran. Beijing confirmed the first formal AI talks between the two countries have taken place and hinted at an extension of the trade truce, with Xi urging Washington to cooperate on AI. Why it matters: no tariff breakthrough means the status quo holds for Philippine exporters, but an AI channel between Washington and Beijing is genuinely new.

Closer to home, the Philippines has accused China of obstructing a resupply mission in the South China Sea, and Taiwan is upgrading maritime patrol capacity for a vulnerable outpost in the same waters.

Three quick ones. Ukraine's President Zelensky says Russia is now targeting data centres to disrupt ordinary life. Crypto exchange Bitget suspects North Korea is behind a 352 million dollar hack. And a US appeals court upheld the Pentagon's designation of Anthropic as a supply chain risk, in a 2-1 decision, while another appeals court ruled states can regulate Kalshi's sports prediction markets.

That's your Morning Briefing for Saturday, September 26. Have a good weekend.
