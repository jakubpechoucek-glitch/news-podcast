Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 17, 2026. Here's your Morning Briefing on finance and geopolitics: Wall Street sold off hard on Nvidia turmoil, the PSEi steadied after a six-year low, and Donald Trump lands in Manila today.

Start with markets. US stocks closed sharply lower. The Dow Jones Industrial Average finished at about 51,608, down 1.4 percent. The S&P 500 closed at about 7,417, down 1.9 percent. The Nasdaq Composite took the worst of it, ending near 24,510, down 2.5 percent. The US 10-year Treasury yield slipped 3 basis points to 5.12 percent, a modest safety bid that did nothing to cushion equities.

Big tech drove the damage. Nvidia fell 4.1 percent to 156.14, the epicentre of the selling. Jensen Huang resigned last week after 33 years as chief executive, and a source tells CNBC the board is not considering bringing him back, despite a "Bring Jensen Back" campaign backed by President Trump and a number of tech figures. Separately, the Trump administration imposed new export licence requirements covering Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore and Brazil, aimed at blocking China's remote access to American AI chips. Alphabet dropped 4.1 percent to 283.72, Amazon fell 3.3 percent to 257.75, Tesla lost 2.7 percent to 379.74, and Meta shed 2.4 percent to 626.41. Microsoft was down 1.1 percent at 517.99 and Apple fell 1.0 percent to 352.78. Why it matters: the AI trade is now hostage to governance and policy risk, not just earnings. Jim Cramer argued the shake-up won't derail the boom; IMF managing director Kristalina Georgieva was blunter, saying the global economy is "standing on one leg" as circular financing among AI companies passes 1 trillion dollars, two-thirds of it debt.

On central banks, there was no Federal Reserve decision. Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh said AI is central to the economy's turning point but warned it could "disappoint", leaving him with hard questions on jobs and rates. Markets are still pricing the possibility of another hike, with the dollar at a 19-month high. From the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, no policy move either: the Manila Times reports the BSP is likely to begin easing only in the second half of next year. The BSP did suspend its dirty-money scrutiny of bank withdrawals above 1 million pesos, and tightened ownership checks on money changers amid the flood control scandal.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi closed at about 5,501, up 0.35 percent, a thin bounce after Monday's 2 percent slide to its lowest level in more than six years, driven by stagflation fears. The peso firmed slightly to 63.74 to the dollar, down 0.19 percent, but still flirting with 64. Why it matters: foreign money is leaving Philippine equities even as regional peers rally, and passive index exposure is being openly questioned.

Three stories to track. First, President Trump is en route to Manila, arriving today shortly before the close of the 49th ASEAN Summit, with the spy scandal and his midterm defeat shadowing the trip. Second, remittances grew in September at their fastest pace in eight months, the main prop under household spending and the peso. Third, Socioeconomic Planning Secretary Arsenio Balisacan flagged costly infrastructure delays, while Marcos ordered the BCDA to fast-track 200,000 jobs in the Luzon Economic Corridor. In corporate news, AboitizPower is taking full control of the 420-megawatt Pagbilao coal plant for 6.12 billion pesos, Semirara publicly slammed the Department of Energy over coal auction conditions, and JICA released 8 billion pesos in post-typhoon disaster funding.

Turning to India. The Nifty 50 closed at about 25,371, up 0.3 percent, and the BSE Sensex at about 82,130, essentially flat, up 0.03 percent. The rupee strengthened 0.6 percent to 93.67 to the dollar. Analysts see consolidation with the Nifty testing resistance around 25,300, and GIFT Nifty points to a flat open as weak global cues bite.

The standout flow story: investors put more than 316 million dollars into BlackRock's iShares MSCI India ETF last week, its biggest weekly inflow since June 2025, as the AI rout pushed money out of tech-heavy markets. Why it matters: India is being bought as the diversification trade, helped by lower crude and improving earnings. Morgan Stanley sees the Sensex at 95,000 by December 2027.

From the Reserve Bank of India, the regulator reiterated in writing that Tata Sons must comply with listing rules for upper-layer non-bank lenders, leaving little room for manoeuvre. Tata Chemicals jumped 9.3 percent to about 669 rupees on more than five times normal volume, on its estimated 2.5 to 3 percent stake in Tata Sons. The RBI is also seeking a review of the Supreme Court ruling on Yes Bank's AT1 bonds, cancelled the registrations of 43 non-bank lenders in West Bengal, and is preparing rules to restrict private equity control of multiple NBFCs. Elsewhere, Aegis Logistics closed a 3,500 crore rupee share placement, and gold and silver sit near multi-year lows on the strong dollar.

Now to the world. In Ukraine, Russia says Kyiv launched more than 2,000 drones in a single day, the largest attack of the war, with the landmark Crimea bridge hit; Kyiv endured a record 17 air raid alerts. At the same time, Ukraine held talks with the US and Europe, with Jared Kushner reporting progress toward a deal, though Moscow has not agreed to European participation in the next round. Why it matters: escalation and diplomacy are running in parallel, and energy markets are watching both.

In the Middle East, Iran reports tankers struck by mines in the Strait of Hormuz, a drone alert at an Iraqi gas field, and the arrival of a US aircraft carrier. Yet US crude fell to 85 dollars a barrel as Middle East exports recover and forecasters project a fourth-quarter surplus. For Philippine motorists, that feeds through to big diesel and kerosene rollbacks this week. In Yemen, the army reports 661 attacks on Houthi forces as the group advances toward Aden, a threat to Red Sea shipping. And in the occupied West Bank, Israeli settlers opened fire in al-Duyuk near Jericho, killing one woman.

Two more. China's slowdown deepened in October, with industrial output, retail sales and fixed investment all missing estimates, leaving Beijing more reliant on external demand just as trade frictions rise. And in the United States, California's attorney general is suing OpenAI, alleging it misled and endangered the public, while JPMorgan Chase blamed a temporary software bug for consumer banking outages, saying it was not systemic or cyber-related. Scrutiny is also widening over Nvidia: Trump-linked accounts traded up to 97.5 million dollars in the stock, and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick faces questions over family ties to the chipmaker.

That's your Morning Briefing for Tuesday, November 17. Trade carefully.
