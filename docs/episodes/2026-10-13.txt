Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 13, 2026. Oil is driving everything today — a Saudi airport attack has lifted crude, hardened rate expectations, and pulled the peso lower.

To the markets. US stocks closed split. The Dow finished at about 51,851, up 0.4 percent, lifted by energy names. The S&P 500 was essentially flat at about 7,807, down 0.1 percent, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.4 percent to about 27,246. The driver was the jump in oil prices after attacks on Riyadh's international airport, which boosted energy shares while weighing on growth and tech. The US 10-year Treasury yield held at 5.24 percent, unchanged on the day. Why it matters: with yields anchored above 5 percent and oil rising, the cheap-money case for high-multiple tech keeps getting harder.

In big tech, Meta was the standout, up 3.4 percent to about 743 dollars after launching its Muse Wonder AI model, which sits behind a paid subscription in the Muse app — the market read that as a test of whether consumers will actually pay for AI. Apple rose 1.3 percent to about 341 dollars, Microsoft gained 1.1 percent to about 541 on reports it will lean more on its own homegrown AI chips and reduce its dependence on Nvidia. Nvidia fell 1.3 percent to about 226 on the same story. Alphabet edged up 0.3 percent. Amazon fell 1.1 percent and Tesla slipped 1.2 percent to about 378, as Elon Musk was confirmed as the biggest known donor of the 2026 midterms, giving 276 million dollars to Republican groups. Note too that the bull market turned four years old on Monday — the S&P 500 is up almost 120 percent from its October 2022 low — while US bank stocks are flirting with correction territory ahead of earnings this week.

On central banks, the Fed is clearly leaning hawkish. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack said higher rates may be needed to stop an inflationary mindset taking hold, and argued restrictive policy is better delivered relatively quickly, through smaller, more frequent adjustments. Reporting suggests most Fed officials see another hike this year, with the timing open; the Mortgage Bankers Association expects hikes in December and again in the first quarter of 2027. Prediction markets think inflation has peaked for 2026 but will stay sticky, with little chance core inflation falls to 2.2 percent this year.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi last closed at about 5,743, a second straight day of gains on bargain hunting in select blue chips, though the daily change isn't available. The peso is the pressure point: it weakened 0.4 percent to 62.88 to the dollar. On the Bangko Sentral, consensus is hardening for more tightening — both Deutsche Bank and DBS now expect two more BSP rate hikes this year, citing hot inflation and the weak peso. Why it matters: rate-hike bets plus rising fuel costs are the two things local brokers say will test the PSEi this week. On that front, pump prices moved again — gasoline and kerosene up, diesel down. Foreign investors turned net sellers of Philippine stocks and bonds in August, a warning sign for liquidity. Government debt service fell to 76 billion pesos in August on lower amortization. BMI, part of Fitch Solutions, raised its bank lending growth forecast for this year but warned higher borrowing costs could slow credit through 2027. And two Filipinos were hurt in the Riyadh airport attack.

Turning to India. Indian equities closed firmly higher — the Nifty 50 up 1.3 percent at about 22,520, the Sensex up 1.2 percent at about 72,472 — recovering after a bruising stretch in which an oil price spike and foreign institutional outflows knocked the Sensex down 548 points. The rupee was little changed at 96.82 to the dollar, holding on to gains after its best single day since April 2, when it rallied 96 paise. That followed an aggressive Reserve Bank of India defence of the currency: the RBI is raising the daily cash reserve ratio maintenance requirement for banks to 99 percent from October 16, and is requiring authorised dealers to hold an FX risk reserve against rupee derivative contracts. Bond yields are near 7.4 percent, the highest since March 2023. The macro backdrop justifies it — September retail inflation jumped to 5.54 percent, above estimates and a near two-year high, which supports the case for another RBI hike. Politically, Rahul Gandhi led an opposition march to the Supreme Court demanding the election chief resign, and Bloomberg estimates Modi's free trade push could add 140 billion dollars to GDP.

Now to the world. The biggest story is Saudi Arabia. Houthi strikes on Riyadh's international airport and a second facility killed 13 people, and the Saudi defence minister has vowed a serious response. The US Federal Aviation Administration has warned American airlines about flight risks over Saudi airspace, and Delta has pushed back its planned first-ever nonstop US–Saudi route from October 23 to March. Why it matters: oil held its advance on fears a widening Middle East conflict chokes regional supply, and shipping stocks including Torm and Hafnia hit record highs as disruption near the Strait of Hormuz lifted tanker freight rates.

Second, Ukraine. President Zelensky says six Ukrainian energy sites have been hit by Russia since President Trump announced an energy ceasefire, and Moscow has stayed conspicuously quiet about whether it has agreed to anything. Separately, Trump is defending his decision to ease sanctions in a Russian diesel deal against bipartisan pushback, saying he made the right choice; Zelensky says it plays into Russia's hands. Why it matters: traders are discounting the ceasefire as a source of oil-price relief.

Third, sabotage and cyber risk in Europe. Poland says two Turkish nationals arrested in Germany will face sabotage charges over a Russia-linked fire at a Starlink ground station. In France, hundreds of supermarkets had their fridges hacked overnight, spoiling millions of euros of food. Britain's AI minister says a breach of UK cyber defences is likely, and Microsoft president Brad Smith argues AI is less regulated than milk and wants global rules.

Fourth, jobs and AI. IMF chief Kristalina Georgieva says AI is hitting the global labour market like a slow-moving tsunami, with young entrants most exposed, as a World Bank jobs council urges crisis-era employment support. And watch Canada: Alberta votes on secession next week, with Prime Minister Mark Carney calling for unity.

That's your Morning Briefing for Tuesday, October 13. Trade carefully — oil is the swing factor today.
