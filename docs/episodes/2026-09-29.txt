Good morning. It's Tuesday, September 29, 2026. Wall Street closed lower as oil and Treasury yields climbed together, the PSEi slipped below 5,800, and a nationwide transport strike shuts Metro Manila classrooms today.

To the markets. All three major US indexes fell. The Dow closed at about 51,482, down 0.7 percent. The S&P 500 finished near 7,684, down 0.8 percent. The Nasdaq Composite ended around 26,820, down 0.9 percent, the weakest of the three. The driver was rates: the US 10-year Treasury yield rose 6 basis points to 5.24 percent. Why it matters: at 5.24 percent, the risk-free rate is competing directly with equities, and it raises the cost of everything built on borrowed money.

Big tech was mostly red. Meta was the standout loser, down 4.8 percent to about 715.62. Tesla fell 3.9 percent to roughly 357.45. Amazon lost 1.4 percent to about 246.15, Microsoft fell 1.4 percent to around 509.22, Apple slipped 0.8 percent to 338.40, and Alphabet was down 0.3 percent at about 342.75. The exception was Nvidia, up 1.7 percent to about 228.86, helped by its new software platform designed to stop AI agents from misbehaving, its roughly 13 billion dollar deal for Hugging Face, and 150 billion dollars of fresh buyback firepower that Jim Cramer says could change the stock's trajectory. Worth noting: CNBC reports nearly half the stocks in the S&P 500 now have a negative beta, meaning the index and its components are moving at cross purposes. Why it matters: headline index moves are telling you less and less about what individual portfolios are doing.

Central banks. The Federal Reserve has raised rates for the first time in years, and Governor Lisa Cook says she sees further inflationary pressures ahead. Short-dated Treasuries are repricing aggressively, the yield curve is nearing inversion, and the 15-year fixed US mortgage rate hit 6.60 percent yesterday. Why it matters: the market is no longer pricing cuts, it's pricing more tightening.

For the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas there is no fresh policy decision, but the forecasts are splitting. UOB sees the BSP holding at 5 percent through 2027 and warns the peso could weaken as far as 66 to the dollar on broad dollar strength, while the Asian Development Bank argues more BSP hikes may be needed to support the currency. Separately, the BSP bill rate fell for the first time since June on strong demand.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi closed at about 5,789, down 0.6 percent, falling below 5,800 as renewed US-Iran tensions pushed oil higher. The peso weakened 0.13 percent to 62.45 to the dollar. Why it matters: a weaker peso plus dearer crude is the worst combination for imported inflation here.

The transport strike began Monday as jeepney and bus fare increases took effect, and in-person classes are suspended today and tomorrow. There is some relief coming: the Department of Energy says diesel prices should drop about 7 pesos a liter, though analysts see only limited benefit from the LPG and kerosene excise tax suspension, and airfares are set to rise in October on a higher fuel surcharge.

On the growth side, a Taiwanese technology firm plans a 1 billion dollar manufacturing plant in New Clark City, and a Swiss challenge is set for a separate 15 billion peso Clark project. The World Bank says better tax collection and more efficient public spending could deliver fiscal gains worth up to 7.1 percent of GDP. Consumer and business sentiment turned less gloomy, and Robinsons Retail trading was halted as the Gokongwei group completed a share transfer ahead of delisting.

Now to the world. First, oil and the Middle East. Markets across Asia fell after President Trump rejected an Iranian proposal, reviving supply fears. Gold dropped to a seven-week low on the stronger dollar and higher crude, and silver lost nearly 5 percent. In the UK, average diesel hit an all-time high of 198.32 pence a litre, blamed on supply disruption from the US-Israel conflict with Iran. Why it matters: energy is now the main transmission channel from geopolitics into Philippine prices.

Second, Washington is threatening to ban US diesel exports to shield American consumers. UK Chancellor John Healey says the government is in talks to stop it. Why it matters: a US export ban would redirect global diesel flows and lift pump prices well beyond Britain.

Third, US-China. Washington and Beijing are lowering tariffs on roughly 60 billion dollars of goods, covering US imports of toys, sports equipment and Christmas decorations, and American farm products going the other way. Trump and Xi met in Washington last week and signalled two more meetings this year, though analysts say tangible outcomes are needed for the truce to hold. Two giant pandas arrived at Zoo Atlanta as a goodwill gesture.

Fourth, closer to home, China conducted naval and air exercises around a disputed South China Sea shoal, and the Philippine Coast Guard says it detected and challenged the Chinese research vessel Jia Hai Ke 7 about 38 nautical miles northwest of Itbayat, Batanes, inside our exclusive economic zone. The vessel reportedly did not respond. Taipei, meanwhile, says Washington understands Taiwan's defence needs. Why it matters: escalation in these waters is the single biggest tail risk for Philippine assets.

Also worth your attention: Mark Zuckerberg and Anthropic's Dario Amodei meet President Trump today as the AI safety debate intensifies. Congressman Ro Khanna will introduce a bill banning recursive AI until safeguards exist, New York City has subpoenaed Elon Musk over AI safety, and Bill Gates has joined calls for legislation. AMD is buying Fei-Fei Li's World Labs for 8.2 billion dollars. Trump announced a 15 billion dollar steel plant with Mesabi Metallics, and SpaceX put its Starship into orbit for the first time. In Europe, 164 people were arrested in French school protests, and Seoul summoned Ukraine's envoy over two captured North Korean soldiers.

That's your Morning Briefing for Tuesday, September 29. Trade carefully.
