Good morning, it's Monday, October 5, 2026. Here's your Morning Briefing on finance and geopolitics: a strong Wall Street close into a hawkish Fed, a flat Philippine market waiting on inflation data, and drone strikes on Kyiv's bridges.

Start with markets. Wall Street finished last week on the front foot. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at about 51,177, up 0.5 percent. The S&P 500 ended at roughly 7,723, up 0.7 percent. The Nasdaq Composite led, closing near 27,191, up 1.2 percent. But the bond market did not share the enthusiasm: the US 10-year Treasury yield rose 4 basis points to 5.28 percent. Why it matters: equities are rallying into the teeth of a yield above 5 percent, which leaves valuations thin on cushion if rates keep climbing.

Big tech did the heavy lifting. Tesla was the standout, up 4.7 percent to about 371, as attention turns to whether Elon Musk can expand the Cybercab robotaxi service beyond Austin after a rocky first month. Alphabet gained 1.6 percent to about 344, Nvidia rose 1.3 percent to about 234, and Amazon added 1.3 percent to roughly 252. Apple was up 1 percent at about 334, Microsoft up 0.9 percent at about 518, and Meta lagged, up just 0.3 percent at about 728, even as it rolled out Muse, an AI assistant that can shop and check out for you with partners including Walmart, Gap, Sephora, Expedia and Best Buy. CNBC flags Nvidia, Micron and a stronger-than-expected jobs report as the three drivers of last week's action. Also note: President Trump has named Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton as his AI czar, putting an intelligence chief in charge of AI policy.

To central banks. The Federal Reserve under Chair Kevin Warsh has raised rates for the first time since 2023, with Warsh describing the move as having "removed a dose of accommodation" — language that keeps the door open to more. Warsh was confirmed 54 to 45, the most divisive vote in Fed history, and that split matters now that he's tightening against the White House's wishes. Complicating things, the Fed has to decide on rates this month without the data it normally relies on. Oil prices spiked after the Iran war, which is the backdrop to the hawkish turn. Why it matters: a hiking Fed with a 5.28 percent 10-year is the single biggest risk to this equity rally. For the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, there's no fresh decision, but economists expect the BSP to hike more than the Fed to support a weak peso, and BSP securities fetched lower yields last week on strong demand.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi was essentially unchanged, closing at about 5,629, down 0.01 percent. The peso firmed, with the dollar down 0.3 percent at 62.50 pesos. The week's main event is the September inflation print: polls point to roughly 6.7 percent, a five-month high, driven by oil and storm damage. Brokers expect shares to stay volatile and possibly extend losses on that, with tight liquidity and sharply narrowing market breadth adding pressure. Bank asset quality is holding: the bad-loan ratio was steady at 3.35 percent in August, though the peso value of soured loans is nearing 600 billion pesos as inflation squeezes borrowers. On the corporate side, GCash has earmarked 5.7 billion pesos of its IPO proceeds for lending, and the Mynt listing is a focus for the market this week. Mitsubishi's 44.5-billion-peso investment in Ayala Corporation brings the group's recent foreign direct investment haul close to 100 billion pesos. Elsewhere, a subsea cable cut between the Philippines and Singapore is disrupting internet services, police arrested 244 suspects in an online scam crackdown, and the government is borrowing 235 million dollars from the World Bank to tackle the jobs-skills mismatch. Watch the weather too: Super Typhoon Choi-wan is intensifying outside the Philippine Area of Responsibility.

To the world. Russia hit Kyiv's Pivnichyi, or Northern, Bridge with a drone for a second straight day, with footage showing a fireball as vehicles crossed. The German chancellor made a surprise visit to the capital. Separately, Ukraine's surprise robot offensive has exposed a vulnerability in Russia's war machine; experts call it not a decisive breakthrough but a sign of how robotic systems are reshaping the front line. Why it matters: strikes on civilian infrastructure in Kyiv keep European energy and defense risk premiums alive.

In the Middle East, all US bombers have left a UK air base over concerns about suspected terror plots, and more tankers have been struck near Iran. Tehran's conditions for de-escalation include a halt to US "acts of aggression," an end to the naval blockade and economic warfare, and the release of Iranian assets. Why it matters: tanker attacks near Iran are the direct channel from geopolitics to the oil price, and oil is what pushed the Fed hawkish. Yemen's president has announced an all-out war to reclaim territory from the Houthis amid an exchange of strikes with the Saudi-led coalition, adding a second shipping risk in the same waters.

In Brazil, polls have closed with incumbent Lula and right-wing Senator Flávio Bolsonaro neck and neck. If no one clears 50 percent, it goes to a runoff on October 25. Wall Street is braced for two wildly different outcomes for Brazilian assets. Why it matters: it's the biggest emerging-market event risk on the calendar this month.

Three quick ones. Ethiopian rebel forces have withdrawn from Mekelle, with pro-government forces taking control of the Tigray regional capital. Supporters of jailed former Pakistani prime minister Imran Khan have launched a long march on Islamabad. And Japan has lodged a formal protest with the United States after a US Marine was arrested over the murder of a woman in Okinawa — a strain on a key Indo-Pacific alliance.

That's your Monday briefing. Watch the Philippine inflation print and the Brazil count. Have a good week.
