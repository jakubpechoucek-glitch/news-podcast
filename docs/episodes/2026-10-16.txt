Good morning. It's Friday, October 16, 2026. Here's your briefing: Wall Street steadied as bond yields fell and money rotated out of AI, the peso closed at a record low, and drone attacks in the Black Sea put oil shipping back in focus.

Start with markets. US stocks finished higher but narrowly. The Dow closed at about 52,178, up 1.1 percent, the clear leader. The S&P 500 ended near 7,744, up 0.3 percent, and the Nasdaq Composite closed at about 26,713, up just 0.1 percent. That split tells the story: traders shifted out of technology and into healthcare and consumer names, with Eli Lilly and UnitedHealth among the gainers, while AI-linked shares stayed under pressure. The US 10-year Treasury yield fell 10 basis points to 5.09 percent, and that drop in yields is what let dip-buyers back into tech late in the session. Gold rallied alongside Treasuries as haven demand picked up, and the dollar had its worst day in more than a month.

In big tech, Microsoft was the standout, up 1.1 percent to about 539 dollars. Alphabet rose 0.5 percent to about 349, Amazon added 0.3 percent to about 255, and Meta was flat at about 737. On the downside, Nvidia fell 1.8 percent to about 217 as investors fretted over AI financing costs, Tesla dropped 1.3 percent to about 362 even as it teased a next-generation Roadster reveal after nearly a decade of delays, and Apple slipped 0.3 percent to 342 despite a new tie-up with Meta to run the Muse AI model locally on clustered Mac Studio systems. The nerve centre of the selloff is debt, not earnings: credit default swaps on AI data-centre firm CoreWeave hit distressed levels, and memory names including SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron were hammered on China risk and fears of a 2029 oversupply. TSMC, meanwhile, posted a record quarter and said chip capacity is now a fierce battleground between Nvidia, Apple, Broadcom and AMD. Why it matters: the AI trade is now a credit story, and credit spreads, not chip demand, are setting the tone. On banks, Morgan Stanley beat estimates with equities trading revenue 909 million dollars above expectations, while Bank of America fell on a revenue miss and a 1.2 billion dollar Treasury fine.

To central banks. There was no Federal Reserve decision, but the signalling is hawkish and unusual. September producer prices jumped 6.3 percent year on year, the biggest gain since 2022 and well above the 5.5 percent consensus. San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly told Reuters that rate hikes are likely needed, but not urgently. Major brokerages now see the next Fed move as a hike in December, and a speech from Kevin Warsh is being watched for further clues. Why it matters: a Fed that is debating hikes, not cuts, into a midterm election is a hard backdrop for emerging-market currencies, including the peso. From the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, no rate decision or policy signal in the last 36 hours.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi rebounded 0.8 percent to close at about 5,663, snapping a two-day slide on bargain hunting after holding the 5,600 support level, having earlier touched its lowest since 2020. The peso, though, remains the pain point: the dollar bought 63.02 pesos, up 0.31 percent, after the currency broke past 63 to the dollar for the first time ever. On the data, the BSP reported OFW remittances hit 3 billion dollars in August, but cash remittance growth stayed below 2 percent for a third straight month. Why it matters: weak remittance growth removes the peso's traditional cushion just as US yields stay above 5 percent. The BSP also adopted a new global standard for operational risk capital, revamping how banks hold capital against fraud and cyber losses. In business and politics, UPS opened an 80 million dollar hub in Clark, its largest Philippine investment to date; the Energy Regulatory Commission lifted capacity limits on new power plants; Robinsons Retail trading was halted as the Gokongwei group completed a share transfer for delisting; and Manila called out China's envoy over a "use of force" remark on the West Philippine Sea. President Trump is set to visit Manila for the ASEAN summit in November.

Turning to India. Indian benchmarks gave back part of a big rally. The Nifty 50 closed at about 22,623, down 0.4 percent, and the Sensex at about 72,782, down 0.3 percent, after the prior session's 1,534-point Sensex surge, the best day in four months, driven by easing crude and gains in Reliance, IT and banks. The rupee firmed slightly to 95.04 to the dollar. From the Reserve Bank of India, bankers estimate the central bank has already spent roughly half of its 140 billion dollar foreign exchange capital haul defending the rupee, and the RBI raised the daily cash reserve ratio maintenance requirement for banks to 99 percent effective today, while clarifying the scope of its new forex curbs after market swings. Why it matters: the RBI is tightening domestic liquidity and leaning hard against speculation, so expect the rupee to be managed, not freed. India's current account deficit narrowed to 5.2 billion dollars in August. The IMF projects India will overtake Japan as the world's fourth-largest economy in 2027 at 4.52 trillion dollars. In corporates, Wipro returned 17,029 crore rupees to shareholders in the first half, L&T Finance profit rose 33 percent, and IIFL Finance profit nearly doubled.

Now the world. Ukraine's President Zelensky said the pause on striking Russian energy sites is over, and Russia promptly accused Kyiv of hitting an oil refinery. In the Black Sea, drone attacks left two chemical tankers ablaze off Romania's coast with seven crew missing. Brent held steady, with the market weighing a possible US easing of sanctions on the global shadow fleet. Why it matters: tanker attacks plus refinery strikes keep a war-risk premium in freight and fuel. Separately, the UN says shipping disruption at Hormuz is now hitting harvests, a fresh food-price channel. In the Middle East, Israel halted commercial goods entering Gaza, including fruit and winter clothing, cutting off a critical supply line, while a Houthi ballistic missile strike on Yemen's al-Maashiq presidential palace killed two, though the prime minister was unhurt. In Pakistan, at least 33 pupils and a teacher were killed when a school roof collapsed through three classrooms. In Europe, torrential rain flooded Istanbul, killing at least three with several missing, and 19 people were injured when a bus hit a stopped train on the French side of the Channel Tunnel. On trade, China signalled rare earths will keep flowing to the European Union regardless of whether the US trade deal lapses, a reminder that supply-chain leverage is being applied selectively. And in the UK, bank executives expect to be targeted in Chancellor Healey's Budget tax plans after the economy grew a surprise 0.1 percent in August.

That's your Morning Briefing. Have a steady Friday.
