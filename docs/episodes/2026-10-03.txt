Good morning. It's Saturday, October 3, 2026, and here's your Morning Briefing on finance and geopolitics. A weak US jobs report lifted Wall Street, Manila hit an 11-month low, and the G7 is opening the oil taps.

To the markets. US stocks closed Friday broadly higher. The Dow Jones Industrial Average finished at about 51,177, up 0.5 percent. The S&P 500 ended near 7,723, up 0.7 percent. The Nasdaq Composite led, closing around 27,191, up 1.2 percent. The driver was the September jobs report: non-farm payrolls rose just 29,000, well under the 84,000 forecast, and the unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2 percent. Why it matters: with the Fed still in tightening mode, a soft labour market takes the pressure off, and equity traders took it as a green light.

The bond market didn't fully agree. The US 10-year Treasury yield rose 4 basis points to 5.28 percent — still elevated, and a reminder that inflation, not growth, is the Fed's live problem.

Big tech did the heavy lifting. Tesla jumped 4.7 percent to about 371 dollars after better-than-expected third-quarter delivery numbers, though its core auto business faces cheaper Chinese and European EVs. Alphabet rose 1.6 percent to about 344 dollars, with analysts saying its new Gemini 4 model puts Google back in the frontier AI conversation. Nvidia gained 1.3 percent to about 234 dollars, Amazon 1.3 percent to about 252, Apple 1.0 percent to about 334, Microsoft 0.9 percent to about 518, and Meta lagged, up 0.3 percent to about 728. On the other side of the AI trade, Cerebras hit a post-IPO low, down 20 percent for the week on Nvidia competition and a lock-up expiry.

On central banks: traders now see little chance of a Federal Reserve rate hike at the October meeting after that jobs print, with the debate shifting to December. The Fed's vice chair said it "may take more time" before hiking again, and another official echoed that. Separately, the Justice Department confirmed it will not reopen a criminal probe into former Fed Chair Jerome Powell over the headquarters renovation, after the inspector general found no grounds for referral.

Closer to home, the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas is widely tipped to end its hiking cycle at the October meeting, though some economists still see the policy rate reaching 5.5 percent by year-end on a firmer peso and picking-up growth. No fresh BSP decision has landed.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi closed essentially flat at about 5,629, down 0.01 percent — but that caps a fifth straight losing day, the lowest level in 11 months, and a 3.4 percent drop for the week, with oil and inflation fears doing the damage. The peso firmed 0.3 percent to 62.49 to the dollar.

Three things to watch. First, the government has declared a national energy emergency as Asia braces for supply strain from the Iran conflict, and a gasoline price hike is expected next week. Why it matters: fuel is the main channel through which this war reaches Filipino household budgets and the inflation print. Second, Mynt — the GCash parent — has set its IPO price at 6.60 pesos per share, the biggest listing story of the year in a market that badly needs one. Third, Manuel V. Pangilinan is tightening his grip on Meralco through a 12.4-billion-peso stake deal with Ramon Ang. Also on the wire: the Philippines and Singapore concluded talks on an updated double-taxation treaty, the government signed a 48-billion-peso ADB loan for universal health care, and net external liabilities widened to 65.6 billion dollars in the second quarter as borrowing outpaced foreign assets.

Now to the world. The biggest market story is energy. G7 nations have agreed to release 100 million barrels of oil and diesel from strategic stocks, a coordinated move aimed at capping price spikes and heading off President Trump's threatened ban on US diesel exports. EU countries are heading into crisis talks, with officials warning an American export ban would hit Europe's growth outlook. The strain is already visible at the pump: UK diesel has topped 2 pounds a litre for the first time, at 200.01 pence. Why it matters: diesel, not crude, is the pinch point — it feeds straight into freight, food and factory costs worldwide, including here.

In Ukraine, Kyiv mayor Vitaliy Klitschko warns intensified Russian strikes on critical infrastructure have left the capital in what he calls a "very dramatic situation" heading into winter. Why it matters: infrastructure attacks keep European energy risk premiums elevated just as diesel stocks are being drawn down.

In France, hundreds of high schools closed Friday as nationwide student protests over teacher shortages and school conditions turned violent, with riot police clashing with students and about 735 schools disrupted. Why it matters: it's becoming President Macron's next domestic crisis, and political instability in the euro area's second-largest economy is a risk markets have learned not to ignore. In Spain, Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez lost a key housing vote after the eviction of an 87-year-old woman, raising pressure for a snap election.

On the Middle East, retired Israeli General Nitzan Alon, a former hostage negotiator, said a Gaza deal was possible "maybe a year earlier" and more captives could have come home alive — a significant admission from inside the Israeli system.

Two quick market-adjacent items. Crypto exchange Bitget says it is "not expecting to recover a lot" of the 387.5 million dollars stolen in last week's hack, having frozen only a tiny fraction. And prediction markets Kalshi and Polymarket are facing scrutiny over whether their reported trading volumes reflect genuine activity. On jobs, Anthropic will spend 100 million dollars training nearly 10,000 AI engineers, while Wall Street demand for "agent orchestration" skills has jumped 1,721 percent.

Finally, Bosnia and Herzegovina votes Sunday in an election that may break its long political deadlock, and Team Philippines has four golds at the Nagoya Asian Games after Jeus Yape won virtual taekwondo.

That's your briefing. Have a good weekend, and we'll see you Monday.
