Good morning. It's Tuesday, the 3rd of November, 2026. Today: American voters go to the polls in the midterms, US forces strike Iran, oil whipsaws, and Philippine pump prices split in two directions.

Start with Wall Street, where Monday's close was a tale of two markets. The Dow Jones rose 0.4 percent to about 51,984, while the S&P 500 fell 0.5 percent to about 7,541 and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.1 percent to roughly 25,164. The split tells you money moved out of big technology and into everything else. The US 10-year Treasury yield eased 3 basis points to 4.89 percent, helped by crude oil selling off more than 7 percent on hopes of US–Iran diplomacy. Why it matters: cheaper oil and lower yields are doing the heavy lifting for non-tech stocks right now.

Among the megacaps, Microsoft was the worst performer, down 2.8 percent to about 507 dollars. Apple fell 1.8 percent to roughly 338 dollars, even as analysts raised price targets after a quarter described as imperfect but good enough; its 2,000-dollar foldable iPhone Duo has sold out, with Apple unsure when more stock arrives, and new chief executive Ternus faces rising memory costs. Nvidia slipped 1.3 percent to about 202, Meta fell 1.3 percent to roughly 607, and Alphabet was nearly flat, down 0.3 percent. On the upside, Amazon gained 2.2 percent to about 246 and Tesla rose 1.0 percent to about 404. Elsewhere in tech, Palantir reported US commercial revenue up 148 percent to 984 million dollars, topping government revenue of 953 million for the first time, and Anthropic's revised filing showed 17 billion dollars of third-quarter revenue against a 23.3 billion dollar net loss and 895 billion dollars of purchase commitments. Why it matters: the AI spending binge is now the single biggest swing factor for index returns.

On central banks, the Federal Reserve has held rates steady, and new Chair Kevin Warsh is cutting how often the committee votes on rates, meaning fewer scheduled decision points for borrowers and markets. The Dallas Fed president is openly arguing for rate hikes. At the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, no fresh decision overnight, but economists warn its recent hikes risk further weakening household consumption and investment.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi slipped 0.07 percent to about 5,676, essentially flat, while the peso firmed 0.15 percent to 63.47 to the dollar. Brokers expect sideways trading this week, with peso and oil risks keeping investors cautious. At the pump, prices split: gasoline jumps 3.50 pesos a litre this week, while diesel falls 1 peso and kerosene 1.90 pesos. Why it matters: motorists pay more, but freight and power costs get slight relief. October factory activity contracted for a second straight month, a warning sign for fourth-quarter growth. Development planners at DEPDev flagged that a reenacted budget poses real risks to the economy, since agencies would be stuck with last year's spending plan. Banks, though, are thriving: system assets rose 9.8 percent year on year to 31.59 trillion pesos in September, with record third-quarter profits. Debt service fell 11.7 percent to 1.65 trillion pesos in the nine months to September. And business groups urged Manila and Beijing to lower the temperature in their maritime dispute.

Turning to India. It was a strong session. The Nifty 50 closed up 1.7 percent at about 23,441 and the Sensex rose 1.7 percent, or roughly 1,200 points, to about 75,693, a near two-month high. The rupee was steady at 94.89 to the dollar. Two drivers: a surge in Reliance Industries after earnings, worth some 19 billion dollars in market value and accounting for about half the Nifty's rally, and that 7 percent slide in crude, which matters enormously for an economy importing most of its oil. Gift Nifty pointed to a further gap-up of around 150 points this morning. On the Reserve Bank of India, there is no new policy move, but a month after its rate hike, banks including Bank of India are lifting deposit rates as liquidity tightens. The RBI also eased export declaration rules for service exporters and freelancers under FEMA, with a 10 lakh rupee threshold. Separately, foreign investors' share of IPO anchor books has fallen to a five-year low, and thousands have been displaced by home demolitions in Assam's Dhubri district.

Now the wider world. The biggest market-moving story is Iran. US media report American forces struck Iran's Qeshm Island and positions in the Strait of Hormuz, described by Washington as defensive and following missile threats. Explosions were also heard at Bandar Lengeh. Yet Vice President Vance says he hopes for a negotiated settlement, and Iranian talks with Qatari mediators drove Monday's 7 percent oil slide. Brent has since rebounded to about 105 dollars a barrel, with Goldman Sachs raising its forecasts. Why it matters: with roughly a fifth of global oil passing through Hormuz, every headline moves Philippine pump prices and inflation. Ryanair has already warned it may freeze pay and raise airfares if fuel rises by 2 billion euros by 2028.

In Ukraine, Russian strikes killed at least 26 people at a school being used as a humanitarian aid centre in a southern village, hit by what Kyiv says were nine 3,000-kilogram aerial bombs. Fighting has resumed in the Kherson region after a six-day truce, the war's longest. Meanwhile thousands of European troops will rehearse a potential deployment to Ukraine in the event of peace, with Western officials stressing the force would not conduct offensive operations.

In crypto, North Korea has denied responsibility for the record 6.3 billion dollar Binance hack that US agencies and blockchain analysts blame on Pyongyang. Users have pulled 3.6 billion dollars from the exchange, and Binance's Philippine partner BlockShoals is assessing the impact on its local regulatory sandbox project. Why it matters: it is the largest crypto theft on record and a direct test of exchange solvency.

And in the United States, it's Election Day. Trump made his closing pitch at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool, appearing to concede Democrats hold the advantage. Markets care because midterms historically precede a strong year for the S&P 500, and because Indian and Asian investors are watching for signals on US fiscal policy, the dollar and Treasury yields.

Three quick ones. Britain and the European Union have agreed a draft deal to scrap Brexit red tape on food exports, the most significant piece of the reset so far. A cyber-attack hit shop freezers in seven European countries, pushing temperatures as high as 50 degrees Celsius. And the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has now killed more than 5,300 people, with the Bundibugyo strain spreading to Kenya and Uganda.

That's your Morning Briefing for Tuesday, the 3rd of November. Trade carefully, and we'll see you tomorrow.
