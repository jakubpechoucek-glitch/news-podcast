Good morning. It's Saturday, October 24, 2026. Here's your Morning Briefing on finance and geopolitics: a bruising week for the AI trade, a jumbo rate hike at home, and a record low for the peso.

Start with markets. Wall Street closed the week lower across the board. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about 1.2 percent to roughly 52,215, a drop of around 600 points. The S&P 500 slipped 0.5 percent to about 7,624, and the Nasdaq Composite held up best, down 0.2 percent at about 25,955. The driver was fresh doubt about the artificial intelligence trade: so-called neocloud operators came under pressure, credit default swap spreads widened, and chipmaker Solidigm pulled its initial public offering. Why it matters: the market is starting to price credit risk, not just growth, into the AI buildout.

In big tech, Microsoft was the lone gainer, up 0.7 percent to about 524.51 dollars. Alphabet fell 1.2 percent to 320.21, Amazon lost 1.0 percent to 248.00, and Meta dropped 1.0 percent to 642.62, capping a 9 percent weekly slide and its worst week in four years. Nvidia eased 0.6 percent to 210.96, Tesla fell 0.6 percent to 372.22, and Apple was nearly flat, down 0.1 percent at 347.73, with reports of cracked inner displays near the hinge on the new foldable iPhone Duo even as scalpers queued outside the Beijing flagship store. Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon all report earnings next week, which will test whether capital spending guidance can steady sentiment.

In bonds, the US 10-year Treasury yield rose 3 basis points to 4.99 percent, still just below the 5 percent line. Sticky inflation and resilient activity are keeping yields elevated.

On central banks, there was no Federal Reserve decision this week, but the Fed meets Wednesday, and Bank of America now expects two more rate hikes in 2026. The Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, by contrast, has already moved: it raised its benchmark rate by 50 basis points to 5.50 percent, and economists at Fitch Solutions unit BMI see another 25 basis points in December. Why it matters: the world's two central banks that matter most to your portfolio are both still tightening, not easing.

Now to the Philippines. The PSEi fell 0.9 percent to close at about 5,729, a second straight selldown as investors digested the BSP's jumbo hike. The peso extended its slide to 63.56 to the dollar, weaker by 0.2 percent, after touching a record low of 63.615 intraday and posting a third consecutive record-low close. Traders say speculators are testing the BSP's tolerance. The backdrop is September headline inflation of 7.2 percent, which is what forced the central bank's hand.

Three other stories worth your time. First, the stage is set for GCash's 92.3 billion peso initial public offering, the largest in Philippine history, with listing targeted this month. Second, the Social Security System has made its first-ever overseas investment, and the Philippine Stock Exchange has agreed with its Singapore counterpart to widen capital market cooperation. Third, Japan Credit Rating Agency sees Philippine infrastructure investment holding near 4 percent of GDP through 2027. And weather-wise, Typhoon Rosal, international name Champi, is being monitored inside the Philippine Area of Responsibility. Why it matters: the peso's record run and a 7.2 percent inflation print mean imported costs keep rising for households and importers alike.

Turning to India. The Nifty 50 closed down 0.5 percent at about 22,688, and the BSE Sensex fell 0.6 percent to about 73,249, with midcaps and smallcaps leading the decline. The rupee weakened 0.2 percent to 94.93 to the dollar. The main pressure point is crude oil near 114 dollars a barrel, which lifts India's import bill and its inflation outlook.

From the Reserve Bank of India, the latest bulletin flags a broad-based increase in food prices and rising inflation risks from the West Asia conflict and El Nino, while insisting the economy remains anchored by solid fundamentals. Governor-level comments went further: he said he's open to using interest rates to curb financial stability risks, and he reiterated that upper-layer non-bank finance companies must list, a pointed message with Tata Sons in focus. The RBI also finalised new operational risk capital rules for banks, effective April 2027.

On corporates, Jio Platforms completed India's largest-ever IPO, a 30,188 crore rupee offering subscribed 22.19 times, with institutional demand at 70.55 times and more than 13.8 million applications. Shriram Finance's quarterly profit jumped 59 percent to 3,677 crore rupees, and Tata Motors' commercial vehicle arm swung to a 2,138 crore rupee profit. Why it matters: earnings are strengthening, but oil and borrowing costs are overwhelming them.

Now to the world. In Jordan, two masked gunmen opened fire at a church funeral in Karak governorate, killing seven people and wounding 34. A manhunt is under way. In France, President Emmanuel Macron called a shooting near Paris a racist terrorist attack after one person was killed and five wounded; the suspected gunman has been apprehended.

In the Middle East, Israeli forces began demolishing Palestinian buildings in Hebron after two Israeli soldiers were shot dead by two Palestinian gunmen at a checkpoint on Friday. And the energy fallout from the Iran war keeps spreading: an oil spill from a tanker strike has washed up on beaches in the United Arab Emirates, with satellite images showing slicks for miles, while Qatar has agreed to buy US natural gas, a sign that the war is redrawing global energy flows in favour of American LNG exporters. Why it matters: crude near 114 dollars is the single biggest inflation variable for both Manila and New Delhi.

On Ukraine, Russia and Ukraine confirmed a localised ceasefire in Russian-occupied Oleshky, which the White House says First Lady Melania Trump brokered; residents there had been eating garden weeds to survive. Separately, Kyiv says SpaceX is still restricting Starlink and its Starshield variant despite President Trump saying limits were lifted, and Ukraine has now partnered with Nokia and satellite maker ICEYE to find alternatives. Silicon Valley Democrats are demanding a formal investigation.

Two more to note. The White House says it is lifting tariffs on some Canadian goods but replacing them with levies on other products, so the net relief is unclear. And American Express is cutting 5 percent of its workforce to prepare for AI, taking a 336 million dollar restructuring charge. Bank of America estimates global data centre spending hits 1 trillion dollars this year and more than doubles to 2.1 trillion by 2028. Why it matters: the AI capital cycle is now visibly reshaping white-collar employment, not just chip demand.

That's your Morning Briefing for Saturday, October 24. Have a good weekend, and stay dry.
